The history of these dogs is quite rich and extends back centuries. Understanding their genetic background helps us appreciate their unique qualities. ### What two breeds make a Saint Bernard?

The Saint Bernard breed did not result from a simple cross of just two distinct breeds. Instead, its lineage traces back to large **Molosser-type dogs** brought by the Romans, primarily the **Alpine Mastiff**. These dogs interbred with **local farm dogs** of the Swiss Alps, which likely included ancestors of the **Greater Swiss Mountain Dog**. Later, **Newfoundland dogs** were introduced to enhance their coats for colder climates.

### The Ancient Roots of Alpine Dogs

The story of the Saint Bernard begins many centuries ago. Large, powerful dogs roamed the European continent. These dogs were part of a group known as Molosser-type dogs. The Romans often brought these dogs with them as they expanded their empire. Roman armies used these formidable canines as guardians and war dogs. They served a vital role in protecting Roman camps and supplies. These Molosser dogs were robust and strong.

When the Romans traveled through the Alpine passes, their dogs came along. These dogs eventually settled in the region. They bred with the native dogs found there. One prominent Molosser descendant was the Alpine Mastiff. This dog was a large and sturdy animal. It formed a crucial part of the genetic foundation for many mountain breeds. The Alpine Mastiff adapted well to the rugged terrain and harsh climate. Its size and strength were essential for survival.

Local farmers and villagers also kept large, working dogs. These dogs performed various tasks. They guarded livestock and helped pull carts. These regional dogs were hardy and intelligent. They contributed their traits to the developing lineage of what would become the Saint Bernard. This intermingling of Roman Molossers and local Alpine dogs laid the groundwork. It created a unique canine population capable of thriving in the mountains. Ancestors of the Saint Bernard needed strength for survival. ### The Hospice du Grand St. Bernard and Early Development

The true lineage of the Saint Bernard began to take shape at a very specific place. This place was the Hospice du Grand St. Bernard. It is a monastery high in the Swiss Alps. Monks founded this hospice in the 11th century. Their goal was to provide aid and shelter to travelers crossing the dangerous mountain pass. The pass was treacherous, especially in winter. Snowstorms and avalanches were common threats.

The monks quickly realized the need for large, strong dogs. These dogs could navigate the deep snow and find lost travelers. They started using the large, local farm dogs they already had. These dogs were descendants of the Roman Molossers and native Alpine breeds. The monks selectively bred these dogs for their size, strength, and innate sense of direction. They needed dogs that could withstand the severe cold. These dogs also needed a keen sense of smell.

These early "hospice dogs" were not yet called Saint Bernards. They performed crucial rescue missions. They would often go out in pairs or with a monk. The dogs would locate buried travelers. They would then dig them out or guide the monks to them. Their powerful build and thick coats made them ideal for this work. They became indispensable companions to the monks. The reputation of these rescue dogs grew throughout Europe.

### The Influence of the Greater Swiss Mountain Dog

As the Saint Bernard breed developed, various local dogs contributed to its genetic makeup. One such group of dogs were the ancestors of what we now call the Greater Swiss Mountain Dog. These dogs were also large and robust. They traditionally served as farm dogs in Switzerland. Their roles included pulling carts, guarding property, and herding cattle. These dogs shared a common ancestry with other Alpine breeds. They were strong, durable, and well-suited to mountain life.

The Greater Swiss Mountain Dog, or dogs very similar to them, certainly influenced the early Saint Bernard. They brought traits like a sturdy build and a reliable temperament. These dogs were accustomed to working alongside humans. Their calm demeanor and intelligence were valuable assets. They also had a thick, double coat for protection against the cold. This helped the developing Saint Bernard breed.

The interaction between these various mountain dog types was natural. Farmers and monks lived in the same region. Their dogs would undoubtedly interbreed. This cross-pollination of genes strengthened the desired traits for the hospice dogs. It helped to solidify the Saint Bernard's size and hardiness. It also contributed to its loyal and gentle nature. ### The Impact of the Newfoundland Dog Cross

The Saint Bernard's journey was not without challenges. During the early 19th century, severe winters hit the Alps. These harsh conditions nearly wiped out the hospice dog population. Many dogs succumbed to avalanches or extreme cold. The monks realized they needed to introduce new bloodlines to bolster the breed. They sought dogs that could bring greater resilience and a thicker coat.

Around 1830, the monks introduced Newfoundland dogs into their breeding program. The Newfoundland breed originated in Canada. These dogs are known for their dense, water-resistant coats and their exceptional strength. They are also famous for their gentle nature. The monks hoped these traits would make their rescue dogs even more robust. The Newfoundland's thick fur seemed ideal for the snowy environment.

This crossbreeding had both intended and unintended consequences. The Newfoundland cross did introduce a longer, denser coat. This helped some dogs withstand the cold better. However, the longer fur also collected snow and ice. This created problems for the rescue dogs. The heavy snow would cling to their coats. This weighed them down and made movement difficult. Ultimately, the monks largely reverted to the shorter-haired variety for rescue work. Yet, the longer-haired Saint Bernard persisted. It became popular as a companion dog. This cross certainly influenced the breed's appearance and genetic diversity.

### Evolution of the Saint Bernard Breed Standard

The formal recognition of the Saint Bernard breed occurred in the late 19th century. Before this, the dogs were simply known as "hospice dogs" or "Barry dogs," named after a famous rescue dog. Interest in the breed grew outside the Alps. People admired their heroic deeds and noble appearance. This led to a desire for a defined breed standard.

In 1880, the breed was officially named "Saint Bernard." This honored the hospice where they originated. Key figures like Heinrich Schumacher played a significant role. Schumacher was a Swiss innkeeper and dog breeder. He meticulously documented the lineage of his dogs. He helped to establish the characteristics that defined the breed. His efforts were crucial in preserving its unique traits.

The first Saint Bernard Club was founded in Basel, Switzerland, in 1884. This marked a major step in formalizing the breed. The club drafted the first breed standard. This document outlined the desired physical traits and temperament. It specified everything from size and coat type to head shape and eye color. This standard helped breeders maintain consistency. It also differentiated the Saint Bernard from other large dogs. The breed gained international recognition shortly after. This led to its widespread popularity around the world. ### Key Characteristics Inherited from Ancestors

The Saint Bernard's distinctive characteristics are a direct result of its diverse ancestry. These traits make them the iconic dogs we recognize today. Their immense size and powerful build come from their Molosser-type ancestors. These ancient dogs were bred for strength and guarding. The Saint Bernard inherited a sturdy bone structure and impressive musculature. This allows them to move through deep snow and rough terrain with ease.

Their gentle and patient temperament also has deep roots. The early hospice dogs needed to be calm and reliable. They worked closely with monks and rescued vulnerable travelers. Aggression was not a desirable trait for their life-saving missions. The calm nature of local farm dogs, likely ancestors of the Greater Swiss Mountain Dog, contributed to this. They were accustomed to working alongside people and animals. This gave the Saint Bernard its famous "gentle giant" personality.

The rescue instinct is perhaps the most famous inherited trait. While specific "rescue genes" do not exist, the monks selectively bred for intelligence and a strong sense of smell. Dogs that naturally sought out buried objects or showed curiosity were favored. Over generations, this selective breeding enhanced their ability to find lost travelers. The Newfoundland cross, though problematic for long coats, also contributed to their hardiness. Their dense fur and resilience against harsh weather were valuable. All these traits combine to form the beloved Saint Bernard. This blend of attributes explains why they are such remarkable dogs.

### Beyond Two Breeds: The Ongoing Story of the Saint Bernard

While we often ask what two breeds make a Saint Bernard, it is clear the answer is more complex. The breed did not simply emerge from a single, isolated cross. Instead, it was a gradual evolution. Ancient Molosser dogs, local Alpine farm dogs, and later Newfoundland dogs all played a role. This blend of genetic influences created a truly unique animal. Each lineage contributed essential traits to the Saint Bernard's character and physique.

Modern breeding practices continue to shape the Saint Bernard. Breeders now focus on maintaining the breed standard. They also prioritize the health and longevity of these large dogs. Genetic diversity is important to prevent hereditary diseases. Responsible breeders carefully select their breeding pairs. They ensure future generations are strong and healthy. This ongoing effort helps preserve the legacy of the Saint Bernard.

Today, Saint Bernards serve diverse roles. They are beloved family pets worldwide. Their gentle nature makes them excellent companions for children. They also continue to be ambassadors for rescue organizations. Their image still symbolizes hope and heroism in the mountains. The story of the Saint Bernard is a testament to natural selection and human ingenuity. It shows how specific needs can shape a breed over centuries. The Saint Bernard's journey is far from over. This magnificent breed continues to evolve.

## FAQ Section

### Are Saint Bernards good family dogs?
Yes, Saint Bernards are excellent family dogs. They are known for their gentle, patient, and tolerant nature. They typically get along well with children and other pets. Their calm demeanor makes them suitable for homes with plenty of space. They are very loyal and protective of their families. They prefer to be indoors with their loved ones rather than left alone outside.

### How big do Saint Bernards get?
Saint Bernards are very large dogs. Males typically stand 28 to 30 inches tall at the shoulder. They weigh between 140 to 180 pounds. Females are slightly smaller, usually 26 to 28 inches tall. They weigh from 120 to 140 pounds. Their impressive size makes them one of the largest dog breeds. They require ample space to live comfortably.

### What is the lifespan of a Saint Bernard?
The average lifespan of a Saint Bernard is typically 8 to 10 years. Like many large and giant dog breeds, they have a shorter life expectancy compared to smaller dogs. Proper care, a balanced diet, regular exercise, and routine veterinary check-ups can help them live a full and healthy life. Health concerns common to the breed can also affect their longevity.

### Why did Saint Bernards carry barrels?
The image of a Saint Bernard carrying a small barrel around its neck is iconic, but largely a myth. This popular depiction likely originated from an 1820s painting. It was then popularized by Edwin Landseer's painting "Alpine Mastiffs Reanimating a Distressed Traveller." The monks at the hospice never equipped their dogs with barrels of brandy. The dogs carried blankets, food, or other emergency supplies, but not liquor.

### Are Saint Bernards easy to train?
Saint Bernards are intelligent dogs, but their large size can make training a challenge if not started early. They are generally eager to please their owners. Consistency and positive reinforcement are key. Early socialization and obedience training are essential. This helps manage their size and strength as they grow. They respond well to gentle, firm guidance.

### Do Saint Bernards shed a lot?
Yes, Saint Bernards do shed a considerable amount. They have a thick double coat, whether short-haired (smooth coat) or long-haired (rough coat). Both varieties shed, particularly during seasonal changes. Regular brushing, at least a few times a week, is necessary. This helps to manage loose fur and keep their coat healthy. Daily brushing is often required during peak shedding seasons.
