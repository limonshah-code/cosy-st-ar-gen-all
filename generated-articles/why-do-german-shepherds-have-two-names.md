## The Historical "Second Name": Alsatian Wolfdog

One of the most significant reasons why German Shepherds have two names ties back to history. During periods of strong anti-German sentiment, particularly around World War I and World War II, the breed faced a renaming. People in English-speaking countries did not want to associate the dog with Germany. This led to a very deliberate change.

The breed was temporarily renamed the "Alsatian Wolfdog" or just "Alsatian." This name sought to distance the breed from its German origins. Alsace is a region in France that shares a border with Germany. The "Wolfdog" part was added due to their wolf-like appearance. It was a strategic move to maintain the breed's popularity and avoid negative public perception.

I can only imagine the impact this had on the breed's identity at the time. A name change of this magnitude speaks volumes about the societal pressures present. Kennel clubs in various countries adopted this new name. For example, the Kennel Club in the United Kingdom officially used "Alsatian" for many decades. This continued even after the wars ended.

Eventually, the original name, German Shepherd Dog, made its return. The German Shepherd Dog Club of America consistently resisted the name change. They worked hard to ensure the original name remained. By the 1970s, "German Shepherd Dog" was largely reinstated across most kennel clubs. This historical shift is a primary reason why some older individuals or texts still refer to them as Alsatians, contributing to the "two names" concept. This historical context reveals how much social conditions can influence even something as simple as a dog's name.

## Registered Names vs. Call Names: A Canine Identity

Beyond historical reasons, another common explanation for why German Shepherds have two names relates to their registration. Every purebred German Shepherd born to registered parents receives a formal registered name. This name is official and appears on all pedigree papers. It serves a very specific and important purpose within the dog breeding world.

A registered name is often quite long and formal. It usually includes the kennel name of the breeder, a unique identifier, and sometimes even names of famous ancestors. This name tracks the dog's lineage, providing a clear record of its ancestry. It allows breeders and enthusiasts to trace bloodlines, verify parentage, and maintain the integrity of the breed standard. For example, a dog's registered name might be "Von Hausberger's Majestic Prince." This name is used for official show entries, breeding records, and health clearances. It is a dog's full, official identity.

However, imagine calling out "Von Hausberger's Majestic Prince" every time you want your dog to come inside! It is simply not practical for daily life. This is where the "call name" comes in. The call name is the informal, shorter name you use every day. It is the name your dog truly knows and responds to. The choice of a call name is personal. Owners often pick names based on personality, appearance, or simply what sounds good. ### The Purpose of Registered Names

Registered names serve vital roles within the dog community. They are essential for maintaining accurate breeding records. This ensures responsible breeding practices. They also help prevent inbreeding and promote genetic diversity.

*   **Lineage Tracking:** A registered name allows you to trace a dog's family tree back generations. This is crucial for understanding genetic health and temperament.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do all purebred dogs have registered names?
Yes, most purebred dogs registered with a national kennel club, like the American Kennel Club (AKC), have a formal registered name. This name is typically longer and more specific than their everyday call name. It includes kennel names and lineage information, serving as their official identity for breed records and shows.

### What is the primary difference between a German Shepherd and an Alsatian?
Historically, there is no genetic difference between a German Shepherd and an Alsatian. The term "Alsatian" or "Alsatian Wolfdog" was a name given to the German Shepherd breed during World War I and II due to anti-German sentiment in English-speaking countries. It was a temporary renaming, and "German Shepherd Dog" is now the universally accepted name again.

### Why do German Shepherds make good police dogs?
German Shepherds excel as police dogs due to their exceptional intelligence, strong work ethic, and trainability. They possess a keen sense of smell, high courage, and unwavering loyalty, making them ideal for tasks like tracking, apprehension, detection of narcotics or explosives, and protection. Their physical prowess also contributes to their effectiveness in these demanding roles.

### Is "GSD" an official name for the breed?
"GSD" is an widely accepted abbreviation for "German Shepherd Dog." While "German Shepherd Dog" is the official and full name recognized by kennel clubs, "GSD" is commonly used in writing and informal conversation. It is a convenient shorthand that refers to the exact same breed and is understood within the dog community.

### How important is a dog's registered name?
A dog's registered name is very important for official purposes within the purebred dog community. It is crucial for tracking pedigree, verifying lineage, entering conformation shows, and maintaining health records. It helps ensure responsible breeding practices and the preservation of the breed's characteristics, even if the owner uses a different call name daily.

### Do German Shepherds still have wolf in them?
No, modern German Shepherds do not have recent wolf ancestry. While all domestic dogs, including German Shepherds, are descended from wolves (Canis lupus) thousands of years ago, they are a distinct species (Canis familiaris). The "Wolfdog" part of the historical "Alsatian Wolfdog" name referred to their wolf-like appearance, not direct wolf blood.
