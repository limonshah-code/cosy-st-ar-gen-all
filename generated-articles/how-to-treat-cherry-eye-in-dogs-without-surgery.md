## Understanding Cherry Eye: What Is It?

Cherry eye is a condition where the gland of the nictitating membrane, or third eyelid, prolapses. This gland normally sits hidden in the corner of your dog's eye. When it prolapses, it pops out and looks like a red, fleshy mass. This condition can happen suddenly. It often appears as a small, pinkish-red bulge near the inner corner of your dog's eye.

The third eyelid gland produces a significant portion of a dog's tears. Its prolapse can lead to several problems. The exposed gland can become dry, irritated, and inflamed. It can also be more prone to secondary infections. If left untreated, this can cause discomfort and potentially lead to dry eye syndrome. Certain breeds are more prone to cherry eye. These include Bulldogs, Beagles, Cocker Spaniels, and Boston Terriers.

The exact cause of cherry eye is not fully understood. It is believed to be due to a weakening of the connective tissue that holds the gland in place. This weakening allows the gland to slip out of its normal position. Trauma or injury to the eye is rarely a cause. It is more often a genetic predisposition. Early recognition of cherry eye symptoms is important. This allows for timely intervention.

Prompt action can often prevent further complications. I know it looks scary, but many cases are treatable. It is vital to seek veterinary advice early. This helps determine the best course of action. You might wonder, "what happens if I don't fix my dog's cherry eye?". Neglecting the condition can lead to chronic irritation, pain, and more severe eye issues over time. It is always better to address it quickly.

## Manual Reduction Techniques for Cherry Eye

Manual reduction is often the first non-surgical method attempted for cherry eye. It involves gently pushing the prolapsed gland back into its proper place. This technique requires patience and a delicate touch. I have learned that the key is to be very gentle. You must never force the gland.

Before attempting manual reduction, consult your veterinarian. They can show you the correct technique and confirm if it is safe for your dog. Your vet may prescribe anti-inflammatory eye drops or ointment to reduce swelling. Apply these medications before attempting reduction. This will make the gland softer and easier to manipulate. You can also apply a warm compress to the eye for a few minutes. This helps relax the tissues and reduces some initial swelling.

To perform the reduction, gently hold your dog's head steady. Use a clean, gloved finger or a cotton swab. Apply gentle, steady pressure directly onto the prolapsed gland. Push it downwards and inwards, towards the back of the eye socket. Imagine you are tucking it back under the third eyelid. Some sources might suggest you try to "pop" it back in, but this is not correct. It is a slow, steady pressure.

Sometimes, after successful reduction, the gland pops back out. This can be frustrating. You may need to repeat the process multiple times a day. If you cannot reduce it after a few attempts, stop. Do not continue to apply pressure if your dog shows signs of pain. Repeated failure to reduce the gland, or immediate re-prolapse, indicates that manual reduction alone is not sufficient. In such cases, you will need to revisit your veterinarian for further options.

## The Role of Surgery When Non-Surgical Options Fail

Despite best efforts to treat cherry eye in dogs without surgery, there are instances where surgical intervention becomes necessary. It is important to understand that surgery is often the most effective and permanent solution. I know pet owners prefer non-invasive options, but sometimes surgery is unavoidable. It is not a failure of your efforts. It simply means the condition requires a different approach.

Surgery is typically recommended when non-surgical methods have been tried and failed. If manual reduction is unsuccessful, or if the gland repeatedly prolapses, surgery is often the next step. Severe inflammation, chronic irritation, or the development of secondary infections that do not respond to medication also indicate the need for surgery. Prolonged exposure of the gland can lead to irreversible damage and dry eye syndrome. Surgery aims to prevent these long-term complications.

The most common surgical procedure for cherry eye is called tacking or imbrication. This involves repositioning the prolapsed gland and suturing it back into place. The goal is to secure the gland so it cannot prolapse again. This procedure aims to preserve the gland's function. The gland is essential for tear production. Some older methods involved removing the gland. However, this is largely avoided now due to the high risk of dry eye.

While surgery is generally successful, it does carry some risks. These include anesthesia risks, potential for infection, and in some cases, recurrence. Your veterinarian will discuss these risks with you in detail. They will also provide post-operative care instructions. Recovery typically involves using eye drops and cones to prevent your dog from rubbing the eye. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can cherry eye heal on its own without intervention?
Cherry eye rarely heals completely on its own without any intervention. In very mild or recent cases, the gland might retract temporarily. However, the underlying structural issue usually remains. Without treatment, the exposed gland can become chronically inflamed, infected, or damaged. This can lead to more serious conditions like dry eye syndrome.

### Are there any natural remedies for cherry eye?
Natural remedies focus on reducing inflammation and supporting overall eye health. Warm compresses can soothe the eye and reduce initial swelling. Some owners use diluted saline solutions for cleaning. Dietary supplements like Omega-3 fatty acids and antioxidants may support eye tissue health. Always consult your vet before using any natural remedies, as some can be harmful.

### How long does it take for cherry eye to go away without surgery?
If non-surgical methods are successful, the cherry eye might reduce within a few days to a week. However, the timeframe varies greatly depending on the severity and duration of the prolapse. It can also re-prolapse at any time. Non-surgical success means the gland stays in place without surgical fixation.

### What are the risks of not treating cherry eye?
Not treating cherry eye can lead to serious complications. The exposed gland becomes dry, irritated, and prone to infection. Chronic inflammation can cause pain and discomfort for your dog. Over time, the gland may lose its ability to produce tears. This can result in permanent dry eye syndrome (keratoconjunctivitis sicca), which requires lifelong medication.

### Can diet help prevent or treat cherry eye?
Dietary support can contribute to overall eye health and potentially reduce inflammation, but it is not a direct cure or preventative for cherry eye. Including Omega-3 fatty acids and antioxidants in your dog's diet may support healthy tissues. A balanced, high-quality diet ensures your dog receives essential nutrients. Always discuss dietary changes and supplements with your vet.

### Is manual reduction painful for my dog?
Manual reduction should not be painful if done correctly and gently. If your dog shows signs of pain or resistance, stop immediately. It is crucial to use light, consistent pressure. Applying a warm compress and anti-inflammatory drops beforehand can help reduce discomfort. Always get a vet's demonstration first.
