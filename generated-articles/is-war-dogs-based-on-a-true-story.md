He often targeted those overlooked by larger, more established defense companies. His approach was unconventional, to say the least.

David Packouz, the film's narrator and protagonist, was a massage therapist. He later joined Diveroli, his childhood friend, in this lucrative but dangerous venture. Their partnership began modestly, but soon escalated into dealing with significant quantities of weaponry. ## The Infamous Afghanistan Contract and Its Fallout

The turning point for AEY Inc. came with a massive $300 million contract. This deal required them to supply ammunition to the Afghan National Army. It was the largest contract ever awarded to such a small and young company. The stakes were incredibly high, promising immense profits but also unprecedented logistical challenges. This single contract would ultimately define their rise and spectacular fall.

The core of the problem stemmed from the source of the ammunition. AEY procured much of the supply from old stockpiles in Albania. This ammunition was a mix of Chinese and Albanian-made cartridges, some dating back decades. Regulations stated that Chinese-origin ammunition was prohibited for U.S.-backed forces. This presented a significant, and ultimately fatal, hurdle. To circumvent this, AEY allegedly repackaged the ammunition. They attempted to hide its true origin, which was a serious breach of contract and an illegal act.

The quality of the ammunition was also a major concern. Reports indicated that some of the cartridges were corroded or otherwise unfit for use. This posed a direct threat to the soldiers who would rely on it. The situation created a massive scandal once news broke. The U.S. government launched an investigation into AEY's practices. This probe focused on the origin of the ammunition and the alleged fraudulent repackaging.

The fallout was swift and severe. The U.S. Army suspended its contract with AEY in 2008. Federal authorities brought charges against Diveroli and Packouz. The case attracted significant media attention, exposing the vulnerabilities in government procurement processes. It showed how easily a small, inexperienced company could navigate and exploit the system. This became a cautionary tale about oversight in defense contracting.

The legal battles and investigations turned their business venture into a public spectacle. The media highlighted the youth of the individuals involved. This added to the intrigue and shock surrounding the scandal. ## Fact vs. Fiction: What the Movie Changed for Drama

While *War Dogs* is based on a true story, it takes considerable creative liberties. Hollywood often exaggerates events for dramatic effect. The film certainly did this to enhance the narrative's excitement and tension. Understanding these differences helps in appreciating both the film and the real history.

One of the most significant changes involves the perilous road trip through Jordan and Iraq. In the movie, David Packouz and Efraim Diveroli personally transport a shipment of Beretta pistols across a dangerous desert. This scene is incredibly tense and pivotal to their onscreen bond. In reality, David Packouz never made this trip. The events of transporting the pistols were handled by third-party contractors. This dramatic sequence was entirely fabricated for the film. It served to illustrate the risks involved without requiring the actual individuals to be in such immediate danger.

The character of Henry Girard, the shadowy arms dealer portrayed by Bradley Cooper, is also largely fictionalized. He is a composite character, representing various illicit figures the real AEY dealt with. There was no single "Henry Girard" who played such a direct role in their major contracts. His inclusion adds a layer of menacing intrigue to the film. It consolidates multiple, complex relationships into a single, more manageable character for the audience. This kind of amalgamation is common in biographical films.

The movie also simplifies the complexities of the arms industry. It streamlines the bureaucratic hurdles and the numerous third parties involved. The actual process of acquiring and shipping arms is far more intricate and less glamorous than depicted. The film condenses timelines and combines events to maintain a fast pace. This allows the audience to follow the story without getting bogged down in real-world details.

Furthermore, the personal dynamic between Packouz and Diveroli is heavily dramatized. While they were childhood friends and business partners, their onscreen portrayal emphasizes their clashes more intensely. The film exaggerates their rivalries and the betrayals that occurred. These artistic choices heighten the emotional stakes for viewers. They provide a clear narrative arc of friendship turning into animosity. The true events, while certainly strained, may not have been as overtly theatrical. ## The Real David Packouz and Efraim Diveroli Today

After the scandal, both Efraim Diveroli and David Packouz faced severe legal consequences. Their story, as depicted in *War Dogs*, ends with their downfall. However, their real lives continued after their sentences. Their paths diverged significantly post-prison.

Efraim Diveroli received a four-year prison sentence in 2011. He pleaded guilty to conspiracy to defraud the United States. His early life was marked by involvement in his family's arms business. This background perhaps prepared him for the industry but not for its legal pitfalls. After his release from federal prison in 2014, Diveroli published a book. It's titled *Once a Gun Runner: The Story of Efraim Diveroli*. This book offers his personal account of the AEY saga. He has largely stayed out of the public eye since. His life now seems far removed from the high-flying, risky world of international arms dealing. He learned a hard lesson about the consequences of illegal activities.

David Packouz received a lighter sentence than Diveroli. He was sentenced to seven months of house arrest and probation in 2011. Packouz cooperated with federal authorities during the investigation. This cooperation likely contributed to his reduced punishment. His book, *Arms and the Dudes*, co-authored with Guy Lawson, was the primary source material for the film. After his legal troubles, Packouz returned to a more conventional life. He has since pursued careers in music and entrepreneurship. He has also engaged in interviews and public appearances related to the *War Dogs* film. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is "War Dogs" entirely accurate to the true story?
No, "War Dogs" is not entirely accurate. While based on the real story of Efraim Diveroli and David Packouz and AEY Inc., the film takes significant creative liberties. Many events, like the perilous road trip through Iraq, were dramatized or completely fabricated for cinematic effect. Character portrayals and timelines were also adjusted to enhance the narrative.

### What happened to Henry Girard in real life?
The character of Henry Girard, played by Bradley Cooper, is largely a fictional creation. He is a composite figure, representing various shadowy arms dealers and intermediaries the real AEY team encountered. There was no single individual named Henry Girard who played such a prominent role in the actual events as depicted in the movie.

### Did David Packouz really cross Iraq with guns?
No, the real David Packouz did not personally undertake the dangerous road trip across Jordan and Iraq to deliver guns. This highly intense and dramatic scene in the film was entirely fabricated for cinematic purposes. The actual transportation of arms was handled by third-party contractors and did not involve Packouz in such direct peril.

### What was the biggest lie in the "War Dogs" movie?
The biggest lie or most significant fabrication in "War Dogs" is arguably the inclusion of the perilous road trip through the "Triangle of Death." This intense sequence, showing Packouz and Diveroli personally driving a truck of Berettas across a war zone, never happened in real life. It was a major cinematic embellishment.

### Where are the real Efraim Diveroli and David Packouz now?
After their legal troubles, both Efraim Diveroli and David Packouz are no longer involved in arms dealing. Efraim Diveroli served a four-year prison sentence and later published a book about his experiences. David Packouz received a lighter sentence and has since pursued interests in music and other entrepreneurial ventures, remaining in the public eye more so than Diveroli.

### Was the "War Dogs" movie based on a book?
Yes, "War Dogs" was based on a non-fiction book. The film draws its inspiration from Guy Lawson's 2011 *Rolling Stone* article titled "Arms and the Dudes." Lawson later expanded this article into a full-length book published in 2015, also titled "Arms and the Dudes," which provided the comprehensive source material for the movie.
