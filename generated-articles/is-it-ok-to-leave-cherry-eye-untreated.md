### Can You Leave Cherry Eye Untreated?

No, you should not leave cherry eye untreated. Untreated cherry eye often leads to pain and discomfort for your dog. It can also cause serious health problems. These problems include dry eye, infections, and even vision impairment. Prompt veterinary attention is always the best course of action.

## Understanding Cherry Eye: What Is It Exactly?

Cherry eye is a common condition in dogs. It happens when the third eyelid's gland prolapses. This gland is also called the nictitating membrane. Normally, this gland stays hidden in the inner corner of the eye. Its job is to produce a significant portion of the eye's tears.

When the gland prolapses, it pops out. It looks like a small, red, fleshy mass. This mass often resembles a cherry, giving the condition its name. It appears suddenly in many cases. Certain dog breeds are more likely to get it. These breeds include Bulldogs, Beagles, Cocker Spaniels, and Boston Terriers.

The third eyelid gland is vital for tear production. It creates about 30-60% of the eye's aqueous tear film. This tear film keeps the eye moist and healthy. When the gland prolapses, its normal function is compromised. This can start a chain reaction of problems if not addressed. You might want to learn more about [what is the cause of cherry eye in dogs](https://cosyhomecreation.com/what-is-the-cause-of-cherry-eye-in-dogs).

The exposed gland is vulnerable to the environment. It can dry out quickly. Dust, dirt, and air irritate it easily. This irritation causes more redness and swelling. The longer it stays out, the more damaged it becomes. This condition is not just a cosmetic issue. It points to a deeper problem with the gland's stability.

### Key Signs of Cherry Eye

Recognizing cherry eye is usually straightforward. The most obvious sign is the visible red mass. It appears at the inner corner of one or both eyes. This mass can be small or quite large.

Other signs often come with the visible mass. Your dog might rub its eye more often. They may blink excessively. There might be an increase in tear production or discharge. The eye itself might look red or inflamed. Sometimes, the dog may seem uncomfortable.

*   **Red, spherical mass:** This is the hallmark sign. It is usually bright pink to deep red.
*   **Excessive pawing or rubbing:** Your dog tries to relieve discomfort.
*   **Increased tearing or discharge:** The eye tries to lubricate itself.
*   **Redness or inflammation of the eye:** The conjunctiva becomes irritated.
*   **Squinting or blinking:** Light sensitivity or pain makes them do this.

It's important to differentiate cherry eye from other eye problems. Other conditions can cause redness or irritation. A veterinarian can give a proper diagnosis. They confirm if it is indeed a prolapsed gland. Knowing these signs helps you act fast.

## Immediate Risks of Leaving Cherry Eye Untreated

Ignoring cherry eye can quickly lead to discomfort for your dog. The prolapsed gland is exposed to the elements. This exposure causes immediate problems. It is not just about looks; it affects your dog's daily life.

The most immediate risk is the continuous irritation of the eye. The gland, once protected, now rubs against the cornea. Every blink causes friction. This friction leads to pain and inflammation. Imagine having something constantly scratching your eye; it would be very uncomfortable. Your dog feels this discomfort constantly.

Another major immediate risk is the drying out of the gland itself. The gland produces tears. When it is out of its normal position, it cannot spread tears effectively. The gland also becomes dry and inflamed. This makes it harder for it to produce tears. A dry gland is more susceptible to damage. This drying can contribute to more serious conditions.

Furthermore, an untreated cherry eye can obstruct vision. If the prolapsed gland is large, it can partially block your dog's sight. This affects their ability to see clearly. It can make them bump into things. It can also make them less confident during play or walks. Reduced vision means a reduced quality of life.

*   **Chronic Irritation and Pain:** The exposed gland rubs against the cornea. This causes ongoing irritation. Your dog experiences constant pain.
*   **Gland Inflammation and Damage:** The gland swells and dries out. Its ability to produce tears decreases. This can lead to permanent damage.
*   **Partial Vision Obstruction:** A large prolapsed gland can block your dog's sight. This impacts their ability to navigate their surroundings. It affects their daily activities.

## Long-Term Health Complications of Untreated Cherry Eye

The immediate discomfort of cherry eye is just the start. If you leave cherry eye untreated, more severe, long-term health problems can develop. These problems can affect your dog's vision and overall eye health permanently. Understanding these risks is vital for any pet owner.

One of the most serious long-term complications is **Keratoconjunctivitis Sicca (KCS)**, commonly known as "dry eye." The third eyelid gland produces a significant portion of the eye's watery tears. When the gland remains prolapsed and inflamed, its function severely diminishes. Over time, the gland may stop producing enough tears. This leads to chronic dry eye.

Dry eye is very uncomfortable for dogs. Their eyes feel gritty and itchy. It can cause frequent blinking, redness, and mucus discharge. Without enough tears, the eye's surface becomes dry and vulnerable. This can lead to corneal ulcers. Corneal ulcers are painful open sores on the eye's surface. They can lead to vision loss if they become infected or scar. KCS often requires lifelong medication to manage.

Another significant risk is **chronic eye infections**. The exposed and irritated gland is a perfect breeding ground for bacteria. Dust, dirt, and allergens stick to the gland easily. This makes the eye more prone to recurring bacterial infections. These infections cause more redness, swelling, and discharge. Each infection further damages the eye. They also increase your dog's discomfort.

Furthermore, long-term inflammation can lead to **scarring of the cornea or conjunctiva**. Repeated irritation and infection can cause permanent tissue changes. Scarring on the cornea can impair vision. It might make your dog's eye appear cloudy. This can affect their ability to see clearly for the rest of their life.

*   **Keratoconjunctivitis Sicca (KCS) / Dry Eye:**
    *   Reduced tear production from the damaged gland.
    *   Causes chronic discomfort, redness, and discharge.
    *   Often requires lifelong medication to manage.
    *   Increases risk of corneal ulcers and vision impairment.
*   **Chronic Eye Infections:**
    *   Exposed gland is prone to bacterial and fungal growth.
    *   Leads to recurring infections, discharge, and pain.
    *   Requires repeated rounds of antibiotics or other treatments.
*   **Corneal and Conjunctival Scarring:**
    *   Ongoing irritation causes permanent tissue damage.
    *   Can lead to cloudy vision or complete blindness in severe cases.
    *   Permanent changes to the eye's structure.

The possibility of these severe long-term issues underscores the importance of treatment. Ignoring cherry eye can turn a treatable condition into a lifelong battle with eye problems. ## When Is Immediate Veterinary Attention Crucial?

While all cases of cherry eye warrant veterinary attention, some situations demand immediate action. Recognizing these critical signs can prevent more severe damage and pain for your dog. Do not delay if you observe any of these red flags.

First, if the prolapsed gland suddenly becomes very dark red or purple, seek immediate care. This color change can indicate that the blood supply to the gland is compromised. A lack of blood flow can lead to tissue death. This makes the condition much harder to treat. It also causes intense pain for your dog.

Second, if your dog shows signs of extreme pain, it is an emergency. This might include constant pawing at the eye. They might hold the eye completely shut. Excessive squinting or crying can also signal severe pain. These behaviors indicate that the discomfort is significant. They need professional help right away.

Third, look for any changes in vision. If your dog starts bumping into objects. Or if they seem disoriented. This could mean the cherry eye is severely obstructing their sight. Sudden vision loss or noticeable impairment requires immediate assessment. Your vet can determine the extent of the problem.

Finally, any signs of infection need urgent attention. Yellow or green discharge from the eye is a red flag. Swelling around the eye that worsens rapidly. A strong, foul odor coming from the eye area. These are all signs of infection. Infections can spread quickly. They can cause severe damage to the eye if left untreated.

## Treatment Options: Beyond Just Leaving It

Once cherry eye is diagnosed, your veterinarian will discuss treatment options. The good news is that cherry eye is treatable. The goal is always to reposition the gland and restore its function. Leaving it alone is not a viable option.

The most common and effective treatment for cherry eye is **surgical repositioning**. This procedure aims to tuck the gland back into its proper place. The veterinarian creates a small pocket or suture within the eyelid. They secure the gland inside. This technique helps the gland return to its natural position. It also allows it to produce tears again normally. Surgery has a high success rate. It is often the recommended long-term solution. My vet explained that surgery prevents future prolapse.

There are different surgical techniques. The "pocket technique" is widely used. It creates a small flap of conjunctiva over the gland. Then the gland is tucked underneath. Another method involves suturing the gland to surrounding tissue. The choice of technique depends on the vet's preference and the specific case. Surgery usually happens under general anesthesia. It is a quick procedure. Recovery time is generally short.

While surgery is preferred, some non-surgical options exist. These are usually temporary or for mild cases. Anti-inflammatory eye drops or ointments can reduce swelling. Gentle massage may sometimes help the gland retract. However, these methods rarely offer a permanent fix. The gland often prolapses again. These treatments might be used to prepare for surgery. Or they might be used if surgery is not immediately possible. ## Preventing Further Issues and Recurrence

After your dog receives treatment for cherry eye, the focus shifts to preventing further issues. It also involves avoiding recurrence. While surgery is highly successful, proper post-operative care is crucial. Some breeds are also more predisposed, making ongoing vigilance important.

Immediately after surgery, your dog will need special care. Your veterinarian will provide specific instructions. These usually include administering prescribed eye drops or ointments. These medications help reduce inflammation. They also prevent infection. It is very important to give these exactly as directed. You will also need to keep your dog from rubbing or scratching their eye. An Elizabethan collar (e-collar) is often necessary. This collar prevents them from damaging the surgical site.

Limit your dog's activity during the recovery period. Avoid rough play or anything that could cause trauma to the eye. Follow-up appointments with your vet are essential. They will check the healing progress. They will also remove any sutures if needed. This careful post-operative care greatly increases the chances of a successful outcome. It reduces the risk of the gland prolapsing again.

For breeds known to be predisposed to cherry eye, continued monitoring is key. Even if one eye is treated, the other eye can develop cherry eye later. This is because the underlying weakness in the connective tissue might be present in both eyes. Regular eye checks at home are helpful. Look for any signs of redness, swelling, or changes in the inner corner of the eye. Early detection allows for quicker intervention.

Managing environmental irritants can also help. Keep your dog's living environment clean. Avoid excessive dust or strong winds that could irritate their eyes. If your dog is prone to allergies, managing those can also help. Allergies can sometimes contribute to eye inflammation.

*   **Strict Post-Operative Care:**
    *   Administer all prescribed eye drops/ointments consistently.
    *   Use an e-collar to prevent rubbing or scratching.
    *   Limit physical activity during recovery.
    *   Attend all follow-up veterinary appointments.
*   **Monitoring for Recurrence:**
    *   Regularly check both eyes, especially in predisposed breeds.
    *   Be aware that if one eye gets it, the other might too.
    *   Look for subtle signs of redness or swelling.
*   **Environmental Management:**
    *   Keep living areas clean to reduce dust and allergens.
    *   Protect eyes from strong winds or irritants during outdoor activities.
    *   Address underlying allergies if present.

My vet always stresses the importance of follow-up care. It ensures the best possible long-term outcome. Preventing recurrence means giving your dog the best chance for healthy eyes throughout their life. It helps you avoid future worries about their eye health.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Untreated Cherry Eye

### Q1: How long can a dog live with cherry eye if untreated?

A dog can live for years with untreated cherry eye. However, the condition will significantly impact their quality of life. They will experience constant discomfort, pain, and irritation. Untreated cherry eye also leads to severe long-term complications. These include chronic dry eye, recurring infections, and potential vision loss. It is not about survival; it is about their well-being. ### Q2: Can cherry eye eventually go away on its own?

No, cherry eye rarely goes away on its own permanently. The prolapsed gland may temporarily retract in very mild cases, but it almost always pops back out. The underlying structural weakness that causes the prolapse remains. Relying on it to resolve naturally is risky. It typically leads to worsening symptoms and increased risk of complications over time.

### Q3: What are the early signs that my dog's cherry eye is getting worse?

Early signs of worsening cherry eye include increased redness and swelling of the exposed gland. You might notice more discharge from the eye, which can be clear or discolored. Your dog may start pawing at their eye more frequently or squinting. Increased sensitivity to light or changes in behavior, like becoming withdrawn, also signal it is getting worse.

### Q4: Is cherry eye always painful for dogs?

Yes, cherry eye is generally painful or at least very uncomfortable for dogs. The exposed gland is sensitive. It rubs against the cornea with every blink. This causes irritation, inflammation, and potential abrasions. Even if your dog doesn't show obvious signs of pain, the constant irritation can be distressing. It often leads to chronic discomfort.

### Q5: Can untreated cherry eye lead to blindness?

Untreated cherry eye can lead to vision impairment and, in severe cases, blindness. The prolonged irritation and inflammation can cause corneal ulcers. These ulcers, especially if infected, can scar the cornea. Severe scarring can block light from entering the eye, leading to blindness. Chronic dry eye, a common complication, also significantly increases this risk over time.

### Q6: What are the risks of treating cherry eye with surgery?

Like any surgery, cherry eye repair carries some risks, though they are generally low. These include anesthesia risks, infection at the surgical site, and recurrence of the prolapse (though rare, it can happen). In some cases, scar tissue can form. Your vet will discuss all potential risks with you. The benefits of surgery usually far outweigh these minimal risks.
