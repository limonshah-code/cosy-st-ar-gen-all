## How to Clear an Airlock in Your Mixer Tap

Have you ever turned on your kitchen or bathroom mixer tap, only to find a weak trickle or no water at all? It's incredibly frustrating, right? This common issue often signals an airlock in your mixer tap. An airlock is a simple plumbing problem. It occurs when air becomes trapped in your water pipes. This trapped air blocks the normal flow of water, stopping your tap from working as it should.

I have faced this problem myself, and I know how disruptive it can be. Getting your water supply back to normal is often simpler than you think. You just need the right approach. This article will show you exactly how to clear an airlock in a mixer tap. We will explore easy, effective methods to restore your water pressure and consistent flow. You will learn the causes, required tools, and step-by-step solutions. Let's get your tap working perfectly again, ensuring a steady stream for all your home needs.

**Takeaway:**
*   Use reverse pressure to push trapped air back into the main water supply line.
*   Bleed isolation valves located beneath your sink to release air directly from the pipes.
*   Address hot water system airlocks with specific considerations for hot water tanks.
*   Prevent future airlocks by turning off and on your mains water supply correctly during repairs.

To clear an airlock in a mixer tap, connect a hose between the faulty tap and a working tap to create reverse pressure, pushing trapped air out. Alternatively, locate and open the isolation valves beneath the sink or on the pipework to slowly release the air bubble, restoring water flow.

## Understanding Airlocks in Your Mixer Tap

An airlock is a common plumbing problem that affects many homes. It happens when an air bubble gets stuck in your water pipes. This air bubble then creates a blockage. Water cannot flow past this blockage, which leads to your tap not working properly. You might see no water at all, or just a weak, sputtering stream. This issue often makes you think your tap is broken, but it is usually a simple air bubble.

Several things can cause an airlock. One common reason is when the main water supply to your house is turned off and then turned back on. Air can enter the pipes during this process. Another cause is when plumbing work is done. Draining the water system for repairs can also introduce air. Sometimes, a faulty water pump or a sudden drop in water pressure can lead to air getting trapped. Understanding these causes helps you prevent future problems.

Identifying an airlock is important for a quick fix. You will typically notice low water pressure or no water from one side of your mixer tap. For example, the cold water might flow perfectly, but the hot water side only drips. Or, you might hear gurgling sounds when you turn the tap on. The water flow may also be inconsistent, sputtering out air before a weak stream appears. These are clear signs that an air bubble is stuck inside your pipes. Recognizing these symptoms helps you act quickly to clear the airlock.

This problem affects the convenience of your daily tasks. A faulty mixer tap makes simple chores difficult. Washing dishes or hands becomes a hassle. Addressing an airlock promptly saves you frustration. It ensures your home’s water system functions smoothly.

## Method 1: Clearing an Airlock with Reverse Pressure

The reverse pressure technique is often the easiest and most effective way to clear an airlock. It involves using water pressure from a working tap to push the trapped air bubble back into the main pipe. This method is simple. It does not require disassembling your tap. You will need a rubber hose, like a garden hose or washing machine hose. Make sure the hose has compatible connectors for your taps.

Here is how you can perform the reverse pressure method:

### Step-by-Step Reverse Pressure Guide

1.  **Gather Your Supplies:** Find a robust rubber hose. It should be long enough to connect your faulty mixer tap to another working tap. Make sure both taps can connect securely to the hose. A kitchen tap connected to a bathroom tap often works well. You also need a bucket and towels ready.

2.  **Connect the Hose:** Attach one end of the hose firmly to the spout of the mixer tap with the airlock. Secure the other end of the hose to the spout of a working tap. Ensure both connections are tight to prevent leaks. You might use tape or a hose clamp if needed.

3.  **Open the Faulty Tap:** Fully open both the hot and cold handles on the mixer tap with the airlock. This creates a clear path for the air bubble to move. Let the tap stay fully open throughout the process.

4.  **Open the Working Tap Slowly:** Now, very slowly turn on the working tap. Start with the cold water side. Let water flow through the hose and into the faulty tap. You are essentially forcing water backward through the system. This creates reverse pressure.

5.  **Listen for Air:** You should hear gurgling or bubbling sounds coming from the faulty tap. This indicates that the trapped air is being pushed out. Water will flow from the working tap, through the hose, and into the pipes of the airlocked tap. The air then escapes through the faucet or back into the main water line.

6.  **Maintain Pressure:** Keep the working tap open for a few minutes. Allow the reverse pressure to fully dislodge and clear the air bubble. You might see some sputtering from the airlocked tap first.

7.  **Test the Faulty Tap:** After a few minutes, turn off the working tap. Disconnect the hose from both taps. Now, try turning on the faulty mixer tap. First, try the cold side, then the hot side. You should see a steady stream of water returning. If the flow is still weak, repeat the process. This method proves highly effective for many airlock situations.

## Method 2: Bleeding Airlocks at Isolation Valves

If the reverse pressure method does not solve your airlock problem, bleeding the system at the isolation valves is the next step. This method involves directly releasing trapped air from the water pipes. Mixer taps often have isolation valves. These small valves are usually located on the hot and cold water pipes directly beneath the sink. They allow you to shut off water to just that fixture, making repairs easier.

### Locating and Operating Isolation Valves

1.  **Find the Valves:** Look under your kitchen or bathroom sink. You will see two pipes connecting to your mixer tap. Each pipe should have a small valve or stopcock. One is for hot water, the other for cold. These are your isolation valves. They might have a small handle or a slot for a screwdriver.

2.  **Prepare for Water:** Place a bucket or a towel directly under the isolation valves. This will catch any water that escapes when you open the valves. Even a small amount of water can cause damage if not contained.

3.  **Open the Isolation Valves Slowly:** First, open the mixer tap fully on both the hot and cold settings. Now, slowly turn the stem of one isolation valve counter-clockwise. You will be opening it. Be very gentle. You might hear a hiss or see a small amount of water come out. This is the trapped air being released. Continue until water starts to flow steadily. Then, close the valve. Repeat this process for the other isolation valve. If only one side of your mixer tap has an airlock, you can focus on just that valve.

4.  **Test the Tap:** Once you have bled both valves, turn off the mixer tap. Then, turn it back on. Check if the water flow has returned to normal. You might need to repeat the bleeding process a couple of times. Sometimes, a stubborn air bubble needs several attempts to clear completely. This method is direct and targets the air bubble close to the source. It is especially useful for airlocks that affect only one water supply line, like just the hot water.

## Method 3: Specific Solutions for Hot Water Airlocks

Hot water systems are often more prone to airlocks than cold water systems. This is due to the way hot water tanks and boilers operate. Heat can expand air, and changes in pressure within the hot water cylinder or pipes can easily trap bubbles. If you notice only your hot water tap is affected, you likely have a hot water airlock. Addressing these requires a slightly different focus.

When dealing with a hot water airlock, the reverse pressure method remains a good first step. Connecting the hot side of your airlocked mixer tap to a working cold tap can often push the air back. Make sure to only open the hot side of the faulty tap and the cold side of the working tap. This creates a strong surge of cold water back through the hot water pipe. This cold water pressure helps to dislodge the trapped air.

If reverse pressure does not work, you should consider your hot water cylinder or boiler. Many hot water cylinders have a vent or a pressure relief valve. Sometimes, opening this valve for a short period can release trapped air from the top of the cylinder. Consult your boiler's manual to find these specific components. Do this carefully, as hot water can escape. Always wear protective gloves and eyewear.

Another effective strategy for hot water airlocks involves the isolation valves again. As mentioned earlier, locate the hot water isolation valve under your sink. Slowly opening this valve allows air to escape. This is a crucial step if the air is trapped closer to the tap. For hot water systems, ensure the main boiler or water heater is on and producing hot water during this process. This helps maintain consistent pressure within the hot water lines. Fixing a hot water airlock brings immediate comfort back to your home.

## Troubleshooting Persistent Airlocks and Advanced Tips

Sometimes, despite your best efforts, an airlock can be persistent. If the initial methods do not fully clear your mixer tap's airlock, do not lose hope. There are other steps you can take before calling a professional. Understanding potential underlying issues helps.

First, double-check your main stopcock. This is the valve that controls all water coming into your home. If it was not fully opened after being closed, it could restrict water flow. Make sure it is completely open. Also, inspect your pipes for any visible leaks. A leak can sometimes introduce air into the system or cause a pressure drop, leading to an airlock. Even a tiny drip can indicate a bigger issue.

If your airlock seems to affect multiple taps or persists after several attempts, the issue might be more complex. Consider if any recent plumbing work was done in your neighborhood or within your building. Sometimes, external factors can cause air to enter the main supply lines. If you live in an apartment, check with your building management.

Another related issue is water hammer. This happens when water flow suddenly stops or changes direction, causing pipes to bang. While not directly an airlock, it often indicates air or pressure problems within the pipes. Clearing an airlock can sometimes reduce water hammer effects. If water hammer is significant, it might signal a larger pressure issue in your plumbing system.

When all your DIY efforts fail, it is time to call a professional plumber. A plumber has specialized tools and knowledge. They can identify the exact location and cause of the airlock. They can also use professional bleeding techniques or check for more complex issues. These could include problems with your water pump or pressure regulator. Knowing when to call an expert saves time and prevents potential damage to your plumbing system.

## Preventing Future Airlocks in Your Mixer Tap

Preventing airlocks is far better than having to fix them. A few simple practices can significantly reduce the chances of air getting trapped in your mixer tap pipes again. Regular attention to your home's water system pays off.

One key preventive measure involves how you manage your main water supply. If you need to turn off the main water to your home for any reason, turn it back on slowly. Do not just open the valve all the way at once. A sudden rush of water can trap air. Instead, open the main stopcock just a quarter turn initially. Allow the pipes to slowly refill. You will hear the water flowing through the system. After a few minutes, open it another quarter turn, and so on, until it is fully open. This gradual refilling helps purge air from the pipes naturally.

Another important tip is to ensure your plumbing system is well-maintained. Periodically check under your sinks for any signs of small leaks. Even minor leaks can allow air to seep into the system over time. If you notice any dripping or dampness, address it promptly. Consider having your plumbing system inspected by a professional every few years. This can catch potential issues before they become major problems.

If you perform any plumbing repairs that require draining your system, remember to refill it carefully. Once the repair is complete, open the lowest tap in your house first. Then, gradually work your way up to the highest tap. This sequence allows air to escape through the higher points in the system. Always open taps fully to allow water to push any residual air out. These simple steps ensure your mixer tap continues to deliver a consistent, strong water flow for years to come.

## FAQ Section

### What causes an airlock in a mixer tap?
An airlock occurs when air becomes trapped in your water pipes. This often happens after the main water supply is turned off for repairs and then turned back on. Draining your plumbing system can also introduce air. Sudden changes in water pressure or issues with a water pump are other common causes for air to get stuck.

### How do I know if I have an airlock or another plumbing problem?
Key signs of an airlock include a complete lack of water from one side of your mixer tap (e.g., hot but no cold), weak or sputtering water flow, or gurgling sounds. If all taps in your house are affected, it might be a main water supply issue. If only one tap or one side of a mixer tap is affected, an airlock is very likely.

### Can an airlock fix itself?
An airlock rarely fixes itself. The trapped air bubble acts as a blockage. It needs an external force, like reverse pressure or bleeding, to dislodge it. Leaving an airlock untreated will usually mean your tap continues to have little or no water flow. Active intervention is almost always required.
