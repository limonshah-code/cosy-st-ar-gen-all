### What is the origin of the phrase "raining cats and dogs"?

The phrase "raining cats and dogs" likely emerged from a combination of old beliefs, urban conditions, and literary influence. There is no single, definitive source. It probably refers to the extreme noise and chaos of a severe storm, possibly combined with the sight of dead animals washed through streets during floods.

## Tracing the Roots: Early Appearances and Popularization

The saying "raining cats and dogs" feels very old, and it is. People started using variations of this phrase centuries ago. Early forms of the idiom hinted at the idea of things falling from the sky during storms. These earlier uses were not always about actual animals.

One of the most famous literary appearances comes from Jonathan Swift. He included the phrase in his 1710 poem, "A Description of a City Shower." In this poem, Swift describes a storm washing various debris through London's streets. He wrote, "Kennels swell'd with dead cats, and dogs were there." This line directly links the animals to the aftermath of a heavy rain. Swift's work was widely read, which helped popularize the phrase. His description offered a clear, if grim, image. It showed how common urban conditions might inspire such an idiom.

### Earlier Forms and Linguistic Shifts

Before Swift, similar expressions existed. Some linguistic historians point to earlier European phrases. These phrases often described intense rain using different animals. For example, some old European languages had idioms about it "raining pitchforks" or "raining old women." This shows a pattern of using unusual imagery for heavy rainfall. The specific combination of "cats and dogs" gained traction in English.

The development of language is a gradual process. Words and phrases change meaning over time. Idioms often start as literal descriptions. Then, they evolve into figurative expressions. The shift from seeing dead animals in flooded streets to saying "it's raining cats and dogs" is an example. It shows how language adapts to describe intense experiences. ## Folklore and Mythological Connections

Many ancient cultures used animals in their myths and legends. It is natural to look at mythology for the origin of this idiom. Some theories suggest a connection to Norse mythology. This connection offers a dramatic and powerful explanation.

Odin, the Norse god of storms, was often depicted with wolves and dogs. These animals were symbols of wind and chaos. His storm maidens, the Valkyries, rode on horses. These horses were thought to bring rain. Some scholars believe that "cats and dogs" could be a corrupted version of these mythological creatures. The roaring wind might sound like fighting animals. The heavy rain could be the result of their powerful movements. This theory suggests a symbolic, not a literal, origin.

### Witches, Familiars, and Supernatural Beliefs

Another theory links the idiom to superstitions about witches and their animal familiars. In old Europe, people believed witches could summon storms. Their familiars, often black cats, were thought to be agents of evil. Dogs were sometimes associated with storms as well. They were seen as harbingers of bad weather.

During severe storms, people might imagine these supernatural forces at work. They might believe the storm was so strong, it was as if cats and dogs were falling from the sky. This theory reflects a time when folklore and superstition shaped how people understood natural events. It shows a darker, more mystical side to the idiom's possible beginnings. These beliefs influenced everyday language.

## The Pavement Theory: Literal Interpretations of Heavy Rain

One of the most commonly cited theories focuses on the practical realities of urban life. Cities in previous centuries lacked proper sanitation. Roads and gutters were open. They collected a lot of waste and debris. This included dead animals.

When heavy rain fell, these open sewers would overflow. The floodwaters would carry everything with them. This included deceased cats and dogs. These animals would then be visible in the swollen gutters or floating on the flooded streets. Imagine seeing such a sight during a downpour. It would be a stark and memorable image. People might then use this image to describe the severity of the rain. The streets became rivers of refuse.

### Animals in Drains and Gutters

Before modern drainage systems, streets often had open gutters. These gutters served as rudimentary sewers. Small animals, including stray cats and dogs, often lived or sought shelter there. Sadly, during extreme weather events, these animals could drown. Their bodies would then be washed out into the streets.

A heavy rainstorm would literally wash out these poor creatures. This would present a grim scene to onlookers. The phrase "raining cats and dogs" could have been a gruesome hyperbole. It described the quantity of animal carcasses appearing on the streets. This theory grounds the idiom in a very tangible and unpleasant reality of historical urban life. It highlights the stark difference between our clean streets today and those of the past.

## Sound Symbolism: The Onomatopoeic Perspective

Sometimes, words or phrases describe sounds. This is called onomatopoeia. Some linguists suggest "raining cats and dogs" might have a sound-related origin. A very heavy rainstorm makes a lot of noise. The drops hitting roofs and pavements can sound loud and chaotic.

Consider the pounding noise of hailstones. Or the deep rumble of thunder. These sounds are powerful. Some people believe the sounds of a severe storm could be likened to the fighting or howling of many animals. The thudding of large raindrops or hailstones might sound like a brawl. This theory suggests the phrase is less about actual animals falling. Instead, it is more about the loud, intense, and somewhat violent sound of a true downpour. It is about how we interpret the sounds of nature.

### The Power of Exaggeration

Language often uses exaggeration to emphasize a point. Saying "raining cats and dogs" is a form of hyperbole. It dramatically increases the image of heavy rain. No one literally expects animals to fall from the sky. The phrase creates a mental image of chaos and intensity. This helps convey the sheer volume of water falling.

Think about other expressions like "storming outside" or "the heavens opened." These phrases also use strong imagery. They are not literal descriptions. They emphasize the power of the rain. The "cats and dogs" idiom does this very well. It makes the rain seem almost alive and overwhelming. This imaginative use of language helps us understand the full force of a powerful storm.

### Animal Presence in Streets

In past centuries, stray animals, especially cats and dogs, were common in urban areas. They lived alongside humans, often scavenging for food. They served a purpose, too. Cats controlled rodent populations. Dogs sometimes acted as scavengers. However, their numbers were uncontrolled.

During heavy rains, these animals could be caught in flash floods. The water would sweep them away. Their bodies would eventually resurface, carried along by the torrent. This grim reality contributed to the "pavement theory." It was a plausible, if unpleasant, source for the imagery. It paints a picture of urban chaos during a storm.
