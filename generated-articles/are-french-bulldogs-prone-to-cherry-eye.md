## Understanding Cherry Eye in French Bulldogs

Cherry eye is a condition where the gland of the third eyelid, or nictitating membrane, prolapses. This gland normally sits hidden in the corner of a dog's eye. When it prolapses, it pops out. It appears as a red, swollen mass, resembling a small cherry, in the lower inner corner of the eye. This gives the condition its common name.

French Bulldogs, like many brachycephalic breeds, have a unique facial anatomy. Their short muzzles and prominent eyes place increased pressure on the eye structures. This anatomical difference directly contributes to their increased risk. The connective tissue that holds the third eyelid gland in place can be weaker in these breeds. When this tissue weakens, the gland can easily pop out of its normal position.

The third eyelid gland produces a significant portion of the eye's tear film. This tear film is important for keeping the eye moist and healthy. When the gland prolapses, it cannot function properly. This can lead to dry eye syndrome (keratoconjunctivitis sicca or KCS) if left untreated. Recognizing this early is vital for the French Bulldog's comfort and long-term eye health. Prompt action helps preserve the gland's function and prevent further complications.

## Identifying the Symptoms of Cherry Eye

Spotting cherry eye in your French Bulldog is usually straightforward because of its distinct appearance. The most obvious symptom is the sudden appearance of a small, round, red mass in the inner corner of one or both eyes. This mass will look like a cherry, hence the name. It emerges from behind the third eyelid.

Beyond the visible "cherry," your French Bulldog might show other signs of discomfort or irritation. You might notice an increase in eye discharge. This discharge can be clear, watery, or thick and mucous-like. Your dog may also rub at their eye with a paw or against furniture. This rubbing indicates irritation.

Your French Bulldog might also squint or blink excessively. This is a natural reaction to the foreign object sensation. The eye itself might appear red or inflamed, not just the prolapsed gland. If you see any of these signs, it is important to act quickly. Early intervention can make a big difference in treatment success.

## Causes Behind French Bulldogs Developing Cherry Eye

Several factors contribute to why French Bulldogs are prone to developing cherry eye. Genetics play the most significant role in this breed's susceptibility. French Bulldogs often inherit a predisposition to weaker connective tissues in their third eyelid. This structural weakness means the gland's attachment is not as strong as it should be.

Their distinctive brachycephalic head structure is another major contributing factor. Breeds with short, pushed-in faces have shallower eye sockets. This anatomical feature puts more pressure on the eye and surrounding tissues. This increased pressure can stress the already weak ligaments of the third eyelid gland. Over time, this stress can cause the gland to prolapse.

While trauma is not a primary cause, any minor irritation or rubbing of the eye can sometimes trigger a prolapse in a dog with a predisposition. However, the root cause remains genetic and anatomical. ## Treatment Options for French Bulldog Cherry Eye

When your French Bulldog develops cherry eye, immediate veterinary attention is essential. The veterinarian will confirm the diagnosis and discuss the best course of action. Treatment for cherry eye primarily involves surgical intervention. The goal of surgery is to reposition the prolapsed gland back into its normal anatomical position.

There are various surgical techniques used to achieve this. The most common and successful method is the "pocket technique." In this procedure, the surgeon creates a small pocket or tucks the gland into the tissue of the third eyelid. Stitches secure the gland inside this pocket. This keeps the gland in place while allowing it to continue its tear-producing function. This technique has a high success rate and aims to preserve the vital tear gland.

Non-surgical treatments, like gentle massage or anti-inflammatory eye drops, are rarely successful long-term. They may offer temporary relief but do not fix the underlying structural issue. For a permanent solution, surgery is almost always recommended. ## Post-Surgery Care and Potential Complications

After cherry eye surgery, your French Bulldog will need careful post-operative care. Your veterinarian will prescribe anti-inflammatory medications and antibiotic eye drops or ointments. These help manage pain, reduce swelling, and prevent infection during the healing process. It is very important to administer all medications exactly as directed.

Your French Bulldog will likely need to wear an Elizabethan collar, or "e-collar," often called a cone. This cone prevents your dog from rubbing or scratching their eye. Scratching the surgical site can lead to complications such as infection or reopening of the wound. Keep the e-collar on your dog until your vet gives you permission to remove it, usually after the follow-up visit.

While surgery is generally successful, potential complications can arise. Recurrence of cherry eye is possible, especially if the underlying tissue weakness is severe. Sometimes, the gland may prolapse again, requiring a second surgery. Another significant risk is the development of dry eye syndrome (KCS). If the repositioned gland does not produce enough tears, or if the gland was removed (which is now rarely done), your dog might develop KCS. This condition requires lifelong management with daily eye drops. ## Preventing Cherry Eye and Promoting Eye Health

Preventing cherry eye in French Bulldogs is challenging because of its strong genetic component. If you are considering getting a French Bulldog puppy, select a reputable breeder. A good breeder will screen their breeding dogs for common health issues, including eye problems. They should be transparent about the health history of the puppy's parents. This practice helps reduce the incidence of hereditary conditions.

While you cannot guarantee prevention, you can promote overall eye health for your French Bulldog. Regular veterinary check-ups are crucial. Your vet can spot early signs of eye irritation or other issues. Maintaining good hygiene around your dog's eyes is also important. Gently clean any discharge to prevent irritation or secondary infections.

Ensure your French Bulldog maintains a healthy weight and receives a balanced diet. General good health supports the body's systems, including connective tissues. ## Living with a French Bulldog Prone to Cherry Eye

Living with a French Bulldog who has experienced cherry eye means being vigilant about their eye health. Even after successful surgery on one eye, there is a risk the other eye could develop the condition. Many French Bulldogs who experience cherry eye in one eye will eventually develop it in the other. It is important to monitor both eyes regularly.

Ongoing monitoring for signs of dry eye (KCS) is also important, especially if the gland's function was compromised. Your vet may recommend a Schirmer tear test during routine check-ups. This test measures tear production and helps detect KCS early. Early detection of KCS allows for timely medical management.

If your French Bulldog does develop KCS, it usually requires lifelong treatment with specialized eye drops. These drops help supplement tear production or stimulate the natural gland. Managing this condition effectively will ensure your dog's comfort and prevent further damage to the cornea. ## FAQ Section

### Can cherry eye go away on its own?

Cherry eye rarely resolves on its own. The prolapsed gland requires intervention to return to its normal position. Sometimes, gentle massage may temporarily tuck it back in, but it often prolapses again. For a lasting solution, veterinary treatment, usually surgery, is necessary to permanently reposition the gland and prevent future issues. You can read more about if [Can cherry eye go away on its own?](https://cosyhomecreation.com/can-cherry-eye-go-away-on-its-own).

### At what age do French Bulldogs usually get cherry eye?

Cherry eye can appear in French Bulldogs at any age. It is most commonly seen in puppies and young dogs, typically under two years old. However, older French Bulldogs can also develop the condition. The genetic predisposition is present from birth, and the physical stress or weakening of tissues can manifest at various stages of life.

### Is cherry eye painful for French Bulldogs?

While the initial prolapse might not be acutely painful, cherry eye is certainly uncomfortable for French Bulldogs. The exposed gland can become irritated, dry, and inflamed. This leads to chronic discomfort, tearing, and rubbing. If left untreated, it can lead to more serious conditions like dry eye or corneal ulcers, which are painful.

### Should I get my French Bulldog's cherry eye removed?

The gland of the third eyelid should almost never be removed. It produces a significant portion of the eye's tears. Removing it greatly increases the risk of developing severe dry eye (KCS), which requires lifelong treatment and can lead to blindness. The preferred treatment is always surgical repositioning of the gland. You can investigate [Should I get my dogs cherry eye removed?](https://cosyhomecreation.com/should-i-get-my-dogs-cherry-eye-removed) for more details.

### Can cherry eye come back after surgery?

Yes, cherry eye can recur even after successful surgery. While surgical repositioning has a high success rate, the underlying genetic predisposition to weak connective tissue remains. Sometimes, the gland can prolapse again in the same eye. There is also a significant chance that the other eye, if unaffected initially, may develop cherry eye later.

### What breeds are similar to French Bulldogs in terms of cherry eye susceptibility?

Other brachycephalic breeds are also highly susceptible to cherry eye, similar to French Bulldogs. These include English Bulldogs, Boston Terriers, Boxers, and Pugs. Certain larger breeds like Beagles and Cocker Spaniels also show a higher incidence. The common factor is often the breed's genetic predisposition to connective tissue weakness or facial structure.
