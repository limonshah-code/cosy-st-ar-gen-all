### Can a Landlord Legally Say "No Pets" in Canada?

No, it is not illegal to say no pets in Canada in most situations. Landlords in most Canadian provinces have the legal right to include "no pets" clauses in their lease agreements. However, there are significant exceptions. Ontario's Residential Tenancies Act generally voids such clauses. Also, human rights legislation across all provinces protects service animals and emotional support animals, making "no pets" rules unenforceable for these animals.

## The General Rule: "No Pets" Clauses in Most of Canada

In many parts of Canada, landlords have the power to decide if pets can live in their rental properties. This is often done by including a "no pets" clause in the lease agreement. This clause means tenants agree not to keep any pets on the premises. If a tenant signs a lease with this clause, they are generally bound by it.

Breaking this rule can lead to problems for the tenant. A landlord can issue a notice to the tenant for violating the lease terms. This could even lead to an eviction process if the issue is not resolved. Most provinces support a landlord's right to enforce these clauses. They see it as a valid part of the rental agreement.

For example, in provinces like Alberta or British Columbia, a "no pets" clause is usually enforceable. If a tenant brings a pet into the unit, the landlord can take action. This action starts with a warning. If the tenant does not remove the pet, the landlord might apply for an eviction order. Tenants should always read their lease carefully before signing it. Understanding these clauses helps avoid future disputes.

## Ontario's Exception: The Residential Tenancies Act and Pets

Ontario stands out from other Canadian provinces when it comes to "no pets" clauses. The Residential Tenancies Act (RTA) in Ontario states that a "no pets" clause in a residential lease is usually void. This means that even if your lease agreement says "no pets," you can still keep a pet. This law aims to protect tenants' rights to have pets.

This specific rule applies to most rental units covered by the RTA. For instance, if you rent an apartment or a house from a private landlord, that "no pets" clause likely holds no power. I have seen many tenants mistakenly believe they cannot have a pet because of a clause. It is important to know your rights under the RTA. This unique provision in Ontario makes finding pet-friendly housing much easier for many people.

However, there are important exceptions to this rule. The RTA's protection does not apply in all situations. For example, some types of housing are exempt from the RTA. This includes certain co-operative housing arrangements or specific commercial properties. We will explore these exceptions in more detail later. Tenants should confirm their rental situation falls under the RTA for this clause to apply.

### Understanding the Void Clause

The RTA explicitly states that any term in a tenancy agreement that prohibits the presence of animals is unenforceable. This means a landlord cannot evict a tenant simply because they have a pet, even if the lease says they cannot. This rule helps ensure tenants can enjoy their homes with their animal companions. It reflects a more pet-friendly approach in Ontario's housing laws.

### Exceptions to Ontario's Rule

While Ontario is generally pet-friendly, some key exceptions exist. The "no pets" clause can still be enforceable in specific scenarios. For instance, if the animal causes severe damage to the property, interferes with the reasonable enjoyment of others, or is a danger to the safety of others, a landlord can take action. This is not about the clause itself, but about the pet's behavior. Another common exception applies to condominium units. Condo corporations have their own rules regarding pets. We will discuss this in a separate section. This distinction is crucial for tenants living in condo buildings.

## Understanding Human Rights and Service Animals

Human rights legislation across Canada plays a vital role in pet policies. Even if a building has a strict "no pets" rule, it cannot apply to service animals. This is a fundamental protection. Service animals are not considered "pets" under the law. They are assistance animals. They help people with disabilities. Examples include guide dogs for the visually impaired or hearing dogs for the deaf.

Human rights codes in all Canadian provinces prohibit discrimination based on disability. This includes the right to have a service animal. Landlords must make reasonable accommodations for tenants with disabilities who require a service animal. This means they cannot deny housing to someone with a service animal. They also cannot charge extra fees for the service animal.

Emotional support animals (ESAs) are a bit different. They provide comfort and support but do not perform specific tasks like service animals. The legal standing for ESAs can vary more by province and situation. However, in many cases, if a person has a documented disability and requires an ESA, human rights laws may also protect their right to have the animal. This protection requires proper documentation from a medical professional. Landlords must understand these human rights obligations. Failure to accommodate a service animal or a legitimate ESA can lead to a human rights complaint. Such complaints can result in significant penalties for the landlord.

## Provincial Variations Beyond Ontario

While Ontario has unique pet laws, other Canadian provinces generally allow "no pets" clauses. Each province has its own Residential Tenancy Act or similar legislation. These acts outline the rights and responsibilities of landlords and tenants. In most provinces, these acts do not override a landlord's right to prohibit pets. This means landlords can enforce "no pets" policies.

For instance, in British Columbia, the Residential Tenancy Act permits landlords to include "no pets" clauses in tenancy agreements. If a tenant signs a lease with such a clause, they must abide by it. Landlords can issue eviction notices if a tenant violates this agreement. This is similar in Alberta, where "no pets" clauses are also generally enforceable. Tenants in these provinces must seek out pet-friendly buildings. They should verify the pet policy before signing any lease.

Quebec has different rules. Its Civil Code does not specifically address pet clauses in the same way as Ontario. However, "no pets" clauses are generally considered valid if clearly stated in the lease. The key is often whether the pet causes undue harm or disturbance. Nova Scotia and other Atlantic provinces also largely uphold "no pets" clauses. The onus is on the tenant to find a landlord who accepts pets. It is always wise for tenants to consult their specific provincial tenancy board's guidelines. These guidelines provide detailed information. They help tenants understand their local rights and obligations.

### Enforcement in Other Provinces

In provinces where "no pets" clauses are valid, landlords have clear steps to follow. If a tenant violates a pet policy, the landlord typically provides a written notice. This notice advises the tenant to remedy the breach. This means getting rid of the pet or finding alternative housing for it. If the tenant does not comply, the landlord can proceed with a formal eviction process. This process usually involves an application to the provincial tenancy board. The board then hears both sides and makes a decision. This emphasizes why understanding the lease is so important for tenants.

## Condo Bylaws and Strata Corporations

Condominium ownership brings a different set of rules for pet owners in Canada. Condo corporations, also known as strata corporations in some provinces like British Columbia, have their own governing documents. These documents include bylaws that all residents must follow. These bylaws can include strict rules about pets. This is a crucial point because condo bylaws often override provincial tenancy laws when it comes to pet restrictions.

Even in Ontario, where "no pets" clauses in rental agreements are generally void, condo bylaws can still prohibit pets. If you own a condo unit, or rent one, you are subject to the condo corporation's bylaws. These bylaws are not covered by the Residential Tenancies Act in the same way. For example, a condo bylaw might limit the number of pets, their size, or even ban certain breeds. Some condos simply prohibit all pets.

These bylaws are usually created to maintain harmony within the building. They address concerns such as noise, cleanliness, and potential allergies. If a condo bylaw prohibits pets, a landlord renting out their unit within that condo building must respect it. The landlord then includes this restriction in their lease with a tenant. Breaking a condo bylaw about pets can lead to fines from the condo corporation. It can also lead to legal action against the unit owner or the tenant. It is essential for anyone buying or renting a condo to obtain and review the condo's bylaws carefully. This helps avoid surprises related to pet ownership.

## What Happens if You Break a "No Pets" Clause?

Breaking a "no pets" clause in a lease can lead to serious consequences. This is especially true in provinces where such clauses are enforceable. If a tenant brings a pet into a unit with a valid "no pets" agreement, the landlord can take action. The first step is usually a warning. The landlord sends a written notice to the tenant. This notice states that the tenant is violating the lease terms. It often gives a deadline to remove the pet.

If the tenant does not comply, the landlord can then start the eviction process. This involves applying to the provincial tenancy board or tribunal. The board will hold a hearing. Both the landlord and tenant can present their side of the story. The board will then decide whether the pet is a breach of the lease. If they find it is a breach, they can order the tenant to remove the pet. They can also issue an eviction order if the breach is severe or not remedied.

It is important to remember that eviction is a legal process. Landlords cannot simply lock a tenant out or remove their belongings. They must follow specific legal procedures. Tenants should seek legal advice if they receive an eviction notice. Knowing your rights and responsibilities is key. Sometimes, mediation can help resolve the dispute without going to a formal hearing. This can save time and stress for both parties.

## FAQs About "No Pets" Policies in Canada

### Q1: Can a landlord evict me for getting a pet after I move in if the lease says no pets?
In most Canadian provinces, yes, a landlord can start eviction proceedings if you violate a valid "no pets" clause. However, in Ontario, a "no pets" clause is generally void, meaning a landlord cannot evict you for having a pet unless the pet causes significant damage, interferes with others' enjoyment, or is dangerous.

### Q2: Do "no pets" rules apply to emotional support animals in Canada?
No. Human rights legislation across Canada generally protects individuals with disabilities who require a legitimate emotional support animal (ESA). If you have a documented disability and your ESA is part of your treatment plan, a landlord must make reasonable accommodation. This overrides "no pets" rules. Proper medical documentation is key.

### Q3: Is a pet deposit legal in all Canadian provinces?
No. Pet deposits are not legal in all provinces. For example, in Ontario, landlords cannot charge a separate pet deposit. However, in provinces like British Columbia, pet damage deposits are permitted. Always check your provincial Residential Tenancy Act or consult your provincial tenancy board for specific rules.

### Q4: What are my rights if my landlord tries to make me get rid of my pet?
Your rights depend on your province and the nature of your tenancy agreement. If you are in Ontario, the "no pets" clause is likely unenforceable. If you are in another province, and the clause is valid, you may need to comply or face eviction. Always seek legal advice from your provincial tenancy board or a lawyer.

### Q5: Can a condo board enforce a "no pets" bylaw even in Ontario?
Yes, condo corporations can enforce "no pets" bylaws, even in Ontario. Condo bylaws are separate from the Residential Tenancies Act. If a condo's bylaws prohibit pets, all unit owners and their tenants must follow these rules. Violating condo bylaws can result in fines or legal action from the condo corporation.

### Q6: What is a "pet resume," and how does it help?
A "pet resume" is a document providing details about your pet. It includes their breed, age, training, temperament, and health records. It can also feature photos and references from previous landlords or veterinarians. A pet resume helps demonstrate that your pet is well-behaved and that you are a responsible owner. This can ease landlord concerns and increase your chances of securing a pet-friendly rental.
