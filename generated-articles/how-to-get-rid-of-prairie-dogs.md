## How To Get Rid Of Prairie Dogs: Effective Control Methods

Prairie dogs, those active, burrowing rodents, seem cute at first glance. However, their presence can quickly cause major problems for homeowners and landowners. These small animals dig extensive tunnel systems, leading to significant damage to land, foundations, and agricultural crops. I know firsthand how challenging it can be to manage a prairie dog colony. Their networks of tunnels, called "towns," can spread rapidly, destroying vegetation and creating tripping hazards.

My aim is to provide you with practical, effective strategies to help you get rid of prairie dogs from your property. We will explore various control methods, including humane deterrents, trapping, and, when necessary, more aggressive removal techniques. Understanding their behavior is key to successful management. This article will guide you through each step, ensuring you make informed decisions to protect your land.

### Takeaway

*   Identify the extent of your prairie dog problem.
*   Implement humane deterrents and habitat modifications first.
*   Consider live trapping and relocation where legal and practical.
*   Explore lethal control methods as a last resort, following all regulations.
*   Seek professional help for large or persistent infestations.

To get rid of prairie dogs, you must use a combination of methods. Start with habitat changes and deterrents. Then, you can use trapping or consider professional removal services. Consistent effort will help you regain control of your property.

## Understanding Prairie Dog Behavior and Impact

Prairie dogs are burrowing rodents that live in vast underground colonies. They are social animals and communicate through barks and chirps. You might see them standing upright, watching for predators. This behavior helps their group stay safe. They mainly eat grasses, seeds, and insects.

Their burrowing habits are the primary reason they become a nuisance. They create elaborate tunnel systems that can stretch for miles. These burrows can undermine foundations, create tripping hazards, and damage irrigation systems. Their constant grazing also destroys vegetation. This makes land unusable for agriculture or livestock. Understanding their nature is the first step in effective control. If you are unsure what these animals look like, you might want to learn [what does a prairie dog look like](https://cosyhomecreation.com/what-does-a-prairie-dog-look-like).

### Identifying a Prairie Dog Infestation

Spotting a prairie dog problem is usually straightforward. You will see numerous mounds of dirt, which are the entrances to their burrows. These mounds often have a distinctive crater-like appearance. You will also observe the prairie dogs themselves, moving in and out of these openings. They are most active during the day, especially in the morning and late afternoon.

Look for areas where grass is sparse or completely eaten away. This grazing pattern is a clear sign of their presence. I often notice paths worn down between burrows, showing their travel routes. Active burrows may also have fresh dirt around the entrance.

### Why Prairie Dogs Are Not Pets

Despite their appearance, prairie dogs are wild animals and make unsuitable pets. They carry diseases, such as plague and tularemia, which can transfer to humans. Their strong instincts mean they will try to burrow, even indoors. This causes significant damage to homes. Many people wonder [where can I buy a prairie dog](https://cosyhomecreation.com/where-can-i-buy-a-prairie-dog). However, acquiring them as pets is often illegal and highly discouraged due to public health risks.

Their care requirements are complex. They need specific diets and extensive space. Pet prairie dogs can become aggressive. They bite, which can transmit serious illnesses. It is always best to leave prairie dogs in their natural habitat or seek professional help for removal.

## Humane and Non-Lethal Prairie Dog Control

When you want to remove prairie dogs without harming them, humane methods are your best option. These methods focus on making your property undesirable for them. They aim to encourage the prairie dogs to move elsewhere. This approach minimizes harm to the animals and the environment. It requires patience and consistent effort.

### Habitat Modification

Changing the landscape can make your property less inviting to prairie dogs. They prefer short grasses and open areas to spot predators easily. Allowing your grass to grow taller can reduce their sense of security. You can also plant dense shrubs or trees around the borders of your property. These plantings create visual barriers. They make it harder for prairie dogs to see their surroundings.

Removing any potential food sources will also help. Keep gardens tidy and clean up spilled birdseed or pet food. If you have a water source, like a leaky faucet, fix it. These small changes can significantly deter prairie dogs from staying.

### Physical Barriers

Fencing is a very effective way to keep prairie dogs out. You need a fence that is at least 3 feet tall. Bury the bottom of the fence 18-24 inches into the ground. Bend the buried part outward at a 90-degree angle. This prevents them from burrowing underneath. Use heavy-gauge wire mesh or hardware cloth. Prairie dogs are strong burrowers, so weak materials will not work.

For existing structures like sheds or foundations, install L-shaped barriers. Dig a trench around the perimeter. Attach hardware cloth to the foundation and bury it. This creates a barrier that prevents burrowing next to your home. Ensure all potential entry points are sealed.

### Natural Repellents

Some natural repellents can make prairie dogs uncomfortable. Castor oil is often mentioned as a deterrent. You can mix castor oil with water and dish soap. Spray this mixture near burrow entrances and on areas you want to protect. The smell and taste are unpleasant for them. It might encourage them to move on.

Predator urine, such as fox or coyote urine, can also be effective. Prairie dogs instinctively avoid areas where they detect the presence of predators. You can buy these products online or at specialty stores. Apply them around burrows and active areas. Reapply after rain or every few weeks to maintain effectiveness. Remember, repellents usually work best when combined with other methods.

## Trapping Prairie Dogs

Trapping is another common method to get rid of prairie dogs. It allows you to remove individual animals from your property. You have two main choices: live traps or lethal traps. Live trapping is generally preferred for humane removal and relocation. However, lethal traps are an option when live trapping is not practical or allowed.

### Types of Traps

Live traps are cages that catch the animal without harming it. They have a door that closes once the animal enters to get the bait. Look for sturdy, galvanized wire mesh traps designed for medium-sized rodents. Sizes usually range from 10x12x32 inches. Make sure the trap is clean and does not smell like humans. You can wear gloves when handling them.

Lethal traps, such as body-grip traps, kill the animal quickly. These traps are highly effective but must be used with extreme caution. They pose a risk to pets and non-target wildlife. Always check local regulations before using lethal traps. I personally prefer humane methods first.

### Baiting and Placement

Proper baiting and placement are crucial for trapping success. Prairie dogs are herbivores. Good baits include fresh vegetables like carrots, lettuce, or apples. Peanut butter mixed with oats can also be very appealing. Place the bait inside the trap, beyond the trigger plate. This encourages the prairie dog to enter fully.

Position the traps near active burrow entrances or along their travel paths. Prairie dogs often follow familiar routes. Place the trap on a flat, stable surface. You can camouflage the trap with natural materials like grass or leaves. This makes it less obvious to the prairie dogs. Check traps frequently, at least twice a day. This ensures captured animals are not stressed for long periods.

### Safe Relocation Practices

If you use live traps, relocation must be done carefully and legally. Before you move any prairie dog, check your local and state laws. Many areas have strict regulations about relocating wildlife. Some states prohibit it due to disease concerns or ecosystem disruption. If relocation is permitted, choose a suitable release site far from residential areas. It should have ample food and water, but also be away from other prairie dog colonies to prevent competition or disease spread.

Transport the trapped animal calmly and quickly to the release site. Release it as soon as possible after capture. Avoid handling the animal directly. Open the trap door and let the prairie dog exit on its own. Releasing prairie dogs onto new private land without permission is illegal. It can also cause problems for others. Consider if professional relocation services are a better option.

## Lethal Control Methods for Prairie Dogs (Last Resort)

Sometimes, despite best efforts, lethal control becomes necessary for severe prairie dog infestations. These methods are potent and effectively reduce populations. However, they require careful planning and adherence to safety protocols. Always verify local regulations before implementing any lethal control. It is vital to prioritize safety for yourself, other animals, and the environment. I recommend trying humane methods first.

### Fumigation

Fumigation involves introducing toxic gases into prairie dog burrows. Gas cartridges are a common option for homeowners. You light the cartridge and place it deep into an active burrow. The gas fills the tunnel system, suffocating the animals. Make sure all burrow entrances are sealed immediately after placing the cartridge. This traps the gas inside. Only use gas cartridges as directed on the label.

More potent fumigants, like aluminum phosphide, are available. These are restricted-use pesticides. Only certified pest control professionals can apply them. Aluminum phosphide reacts with moisture to release phosphine gas, which is highly toxic. It is extremely effective but poses severe risks if misused. Always consider safety and environmental impacts before choosing this method.

### Shooting

Shooting is a direct and immediate method for reducing prairie dog numbers. It is most effective in open, rural areas with clear lines of sight. This method requires a high level of skill and adherence to safety guidelines. Always use appropriate firearms and ammunition. Confirm you have a safe backdrop for every shot. Bullet trajectories can travel far.

Local ordinances and state laws regarding firearms and hunting must be strictly followed. You may need a hunting license or specific permits. Shooting is not suitable for suburban or residential areas due to safety concerns. Many landowners hire professional sharpshooters for large-scale eradication. They possess the necessary expertise and equipment. They also understand local regulations.

## Preventing Future Prairie Dog Infestations

Once you get rid of prairie dogs, the next important step is to prevent them from returning. Prairie dogs are persistent. They will likely try to re-establish their colonies if conditions remain favorable. Long-term prevention strategies are crucial. They involve ongoing vigilance and proactive land management.

### Ongoing Monitoring

Regularly inspect your property for signs of new prairie dog activity. Walk your land often. Look for new burrows, fresh mounds of dirt, or visible prairie dogs. Early detection is key. It allows you to address new invaders before they establish a large colony. If you spot a single burrow, fill it in immediately. This simple act can deter a new pair from settling.

Pay close attention to fence lines and property borders. These are common entry points. Teach your family members to recognize signs of prairie dogs. Everyone can help keep an eye out. This collective effort makes monitoring more effective.

### Land Management Practices

Modify your landscape to make it less attractive to prairie dogs. They prefer short, open grass. Maintain taller grass in areas not used for agriculture or recreation. This reduces their visibility and sense of security. Planting dense vegetation like shrubs or tall native grasses can act as a natural barrier. These plants also provide habitat for beneficial wildlife.

Remove any readily available food sources. Keep fallen birdseed picked up. Store pet food in sealed containers. Ensure there are no leaky irrigation pipes or standing water. These practices reduce the appeal of your property. They make it a less desirable home for prairie dogs. Consistent land management is the best defense against future infestations.

## FAQ Section

### Are prairie dogs a protected species?

The protection status of prairie dogs varies widely by state and specific species. Some species, like the Utah prairie dog, are federally protected. Others are considered pests in many areas, and their control is permitted. Always check with your state's wildlife agency or local authorities before taking any action. This ensures you follow all legal guidelines.

### Do prairie dogs carry diseases?

Yes, prairie dogs can carry several diseases. The most concerning is sylvatic plague, which can transfer to humans and pets. They can also carry tularemia and monkeypox. Due to these health risks, direct contact with prairie dogs should be avoided. Professionals take precautions when handling these animals.

### What time of year are prairie dogs most active?

Prairie dogs are most active during the warmer months, typically from spring through fall. During these periods, they are busy foraging, expanding burrows, and raising their young. Their activity decreases in winter, when they may enter a state of torpor or semi-hibernation, especially in colder climates.

### How long does it take to get rid of prairie dogs?

The time it takes to get rid of prairie dogs depends on the size of the infestation and the methods used. Small, new problems might be resolved in weeks. Large, established colonies can take months or even years of consistent effort. Professional services can often provide faster results. Ongoing vigilance is always necessary for long-term control.

### Can prairie dogs damage my house foundation?

Yes, prairie dogs can damage house foundations. Their extensive burrowing can undermine soil stability around structures. This creates voids and can lead to cracks in foundations, sidewalks, and driveways. The damage might not be immediately visible but can worsen over time. It is important to address their presence near structures quickly.
