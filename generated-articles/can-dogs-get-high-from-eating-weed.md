## Can Dogs Get High From Eating Weed? A Pet Parent's Guide

Imagine coming home to find your beloved dog behaving strangely. They might be wobbly, confused, or unusually quiet. A terrifying thought might cross your mind: could they have gotten into something harmful? If cannabis is present in your home, one urgent question might arise: **can dogs get high from eating weed?** The straightforward answer is a resounding yes. Dogs are highly sensitive to the compounds found in cannabis, and even small amounts can cause significant effects.

As a dedicated pet parent, I understand your concern. My goal with this article is to provide you with a comprehensive understanding of cannabis intoxication in dogs. We will explore how cannabis affects dogs, recognize the common signs of exposure, discuss factors influencing severity, and outline crucial steps for treatment and prevention. You will learn what to do if your dog eats weed, ensuring you are prepared for such an emergency.

### Takeaway

*   Dogs can indeed get high from eating weed due to their unique physiology.
*   Cannabis intoxication in dogs ranges from mild to severe, depending on dose and potency.
*   Recognize symptoms like lethargy, uncoordination, dilated pupils, and urinary incontinence.
*   Seek immediate veterinary attention if you suspect your dog ingested cannabis.
*   Prevention through secure storage is the best way to protect your pet.

### Clear Answer to the Main Query

Yes, dogs can absolutely get high from eating weed. Their bodies process THC, the psychoactive compound in cannabis, differently and much more intensely than humans. This means even small amounts can lead to intoxication, causing a range of concerning and potentially dangerous symptoms that require immediate veterinary attention.

## Understanding How Cannabis Affects Your Dog

When we talk about dogs getting high from eating weed, we are primarily referring to the effects of Delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol, or THC. This is the main psychoactive component in the cannabis plant. Dogs have a much higher concentration of cannabinoid receptors in their brains compared to humans. These receptors are where THC binds, producing its effects.

Because dogs have so many more of these specific receptors, they are much more sensitive to THC. This means that a dose of cannabis that might barely affect a human could cause significant intoxication in a dog. Their system processes the compound very differently, amplifying the effects and leading to what veterinarians call cannabis toxicity or marijuana intoxication. It is a serious concern for any pet owner.

The plant material itself, even without active THC (like seeds or stalks), can also cause gastrointestinal upset if eaten in large quantities. However, the "high" and dangerous symptoms come directly from the THC. Understanding this unique sensitivity is the first step in protecting your furry friend from accidental exposure.

### The Science Behind Canine Sensitivity to THC

Dogs possess a remarkable abundance of cannabinoid receptors, particularly in their brain's cerebellum. This part of the brain controls balance and coordination. When THC enters a dog's system, it overstimulates these receptors. This overstimulation disrupts normal brain function, leading to the characteristic symptoms of cannabis intoxication.

The effects can vary widely depending on the amount ingested and the potency of the cannabis. A small nibble of a low-THC plant might only cause mild lethargy. However, eating a highly concentrated edible can result in severe symptoms like seizures or even coma. The way a dog metabolizes THC also plays a role. Their liver enzymes process it slowly, meaning the effects can last for a prolonged period. This explains why a dog might remain intoxicated for many hours, sometimes even days, after exposure. ## Common Ways Dogs Get Exposed to Cannabis

Dogs are naturally curious creatures. They explore their world through their noses and mouths. This innate curiosity often leads them to ingest things they shouldn't, including cannabis. Many pet owners might not realize the various forms cannabis can take, making accidental ingestion more common than one might think.

The most frequent scenario involves dogs eating cannabis products directly. This often happens when cannabis is left within reach. Think about a dog sniffing out a discarded joint butt on a walk or finding edibles left on a coffee table. Their keen sense of smell makes them experts at finding hidden treats, even if those treats are harmful.

Understanding these common exposure routes is vital for prevention. Knowing where the risks lie helps pet owners secure potential hazards. It is not just about keeping cannabis out of sight; it is about keeping it truly out of reach.

### Ingesting Plant Material

Dogs can easily eat raw cannabis plant material. This might include dried buds, leaves, or stems. Sometimes, this happens if a dog gets into a personal stash left unsecured. Other times, it could be from discarded material found outdoors. The potency of raw plant material varies greatly.

Even if the plant material does not look particularly appetizing to us, a dog's instinct to chew and explore can lead them to consume it. The less processed the form, the more likely a dog might mistake it for something natural to chew on. This form of ingestion often leads to symptoms that appear a bit slower than with edibles.

### Eating Cannabis Edibles

Cannabis edibles pose a significant threat to dogs. These products are often highly concentrated with THC and come in tempting forms like brownies, cookies, candies, or gummies. Dogs are powerfully attracted to the sugar and fat content in these human treats. They do not distinguish between a regular brownie and one infused with cannabis.

The danger with edibles is two-fold. First, they typically contain a much higher concentration of THC per serving than raw plant material. Second, the fats in edibles can increase the absorption of THC in the dog's digestive system, leading to more rapid and severe intoxication. Many veterinary emergencies involve dogs that have consumed edibles.

### Secondhand Smoke Inhalation

While less common than direct ingestion, dogs can also get high from inhaling secondhand cannabis smoke. This usually occurs in poorly ventilated areas where someone is smoking cannabis. Dogs have sensitive respiratory systems, and their smaller body size means they are more susceptible to airborne toxins.

Symptoms from smoke inhalation are generally milder than from ingestion. However, repeated exposure or exposure to very dense smoke can still cause respiratory irritation and mild signs of intoxication. It is crucial to ensure your pets are in a separate, well-ventilated area if cannabis smoke is present to protect their lung health and prevent unintended exposure.

## Recognizing the Signs of Canine Cannabis Intoxication

If your dog has ingested weed, their behavior will likely change. Recognizing these signs quickly is crucial for their well-being. Symptoms can appear anywhere from 30 minutes to a few hours after ingestion, depending on the form of cannabis and the dog's metabolism. The signs often start subtle and can progress if left untreated.

Pet owners might notice their dog acting "off" or unusually quiet. They might seem disoriented or unbalanced. Do not dismiss these changes. Instead, observe closely and consider any potential exposures. Early recognition allows for prompt veterinary intervention, which improves the prognosis for your pet.

Remember, every dog reacts differently. The severity of signs also depends on the amount of THC consumed and the dog's size. My general advice is always: if in doubt, contact your veterinarian immediately. It is always better to be safe than sorry when it comes to your pet's health.

### Common Behavioral and Physical Symptoms

One of the most common signs you might observe is ataxia, which means a lack of coordination. Your dog might stumble, sway, or have difficulty walking in a straight line. They might look "drunk." Another frequent symptom is lethargy or profound sedation. Your dog might become unusually sleepy or unresponsive.

Other signs include:

*   **Dilated Pupils:** Your dog's pupils may appear much larger than normal.
*   **Urinary Incontinence:** They might dribble urine or have accidents inside, even if house-trained. This is a common and distinct symptom of cannabis toxicity.
*   **Vomiting and Diarrhea:** Gastrointestinal upset can occur, especially with edibles.
*   **Tremors or Muscle Twitching:** In more severe cases, uncontrolled muscle movements may be present.
*   **Hypothermia or Hyperthermia:** Their body temperature might drop or rise abnormally.
*   **Bradycardia:** An abnormally slow heart rate is also a common indicator.
*   **Hypersalivation:** Excessive drooling might be noticed.

### Severe Symptoms Requiring Immediate Attention

In some severe cases, especially with high-potency cannabis or large ingestions, dogs can experience more critical symptoms. These include:

*   **Seizures:** Uncontrolled electrical activity in the brain leading to convulsions.
*   **Coma:** A state of deep unconsciousness and unresponsiveness.
*   **Respiratory Depression:** Difficulty breathing or very shallow breathing.

If you observe any of these severe signs, consider it a medical emergency. You must transport your dog to an emergency veterinary clinic at once. Do not delay. Prompt medical care can be life-saving. ## Factors Influencing the Severity of Intoxication

The severity of cannabis intoxication in dogs is not uniform. Several factors determine how strongly your dog will react and how long the effects will last. Understanding these variables can help you provide crucial information to your veterinarian and better anticipate the course of the intoxication. It is never just about "if" your dog gets high, but "how high" and for how long.

Knowing these factors helps underscore why every suspected exposure requires a veterinary assessment. Even if you think your dog only got a tiny amount, their individual sensitivity and the product's potency could lead to unexpected severe reactions. There is no safe amount of recreational cannabis for a dog.

### Amount and Potency of Cannabis

This is perhaps the most significant factor. A small amount of low-THC cannabis will likely cause milder symptoms than a large amount of highly potent weed. Modern cannabis strains and concentrates (like dabs or oils) contain significantly higher THC levels than cannabis from decades past. Edibles are particularly dangerous because they often contain very high, concentrated doses of THC.

A small dog eating a cannabis-infused cookie designed for human consumption is at much higher risk for severe toxicity than a large dog sniffing a tiny, discarded leaf. This is why it is so important to estimate, if possible, what your dog might have ingested. Provide this information to your vet.

### Dog's Size, Breed, and Health

A dog's body weight plays a critical role. A small chihuahua will be much more affected by the same amount of THC than a large Great Dane. This is a matter of dose per kilogram of body weight. Smaller dogs have less body mass to distribute the drug, leading to higher concentrations in their system.

Individual sensitivity also varies, much like in humans. Some dogs may be more sensitive than others, regardless of size. Pre-existing health conditions, especially liver or kidney issues, can also affect how a dog processes and eliminates THC, potentially prolonging its effects or worsening the outcome. Very young puppies or elderly dogs, and those with compromised immune systems, are also at higher risk for severe reactions.

### Form of Ingestion

The way cannabis enters your dog's system also impacts intoxication. As discussed, edibles are particularly problematic because of their high THC concentration and the fat content, which enhances absorption. Ingesting raw plant material might lead to slower absorption, while concentrated oils can be absorbed more rapidly. Inhalation of smoke typically leads to milder, more transient effects compared to ingestion.

The duration of the high also varies. For example, if your dog ate edibles, the effects might take longer to appear but can last much longer, sometimes up to 72 hours, due to slower digestion and absorption. Raw plant material might cause symptoms within an hour or two, lasting 12-24 hours. Understanding this helps set expectations for recovery and treatment. ## Immediate Actions to Take if Your Dog Eats Weed

Discovering your dog has eaten weed can be a frightening experience. My primary advice is always to stay calm. Panic does not help your pet. Your swift and calm action, however, can make a significant difference in their outcome. Remember, the first step is always to contact a professional.

Do not try to guess the severity or attempt home remedies without veterinary guidance. Many seemingly helpful interventions can actually cause more harm. Time is often of the essence, especially if a large amount or highly potent cannabis was ingested. Your quick response ensures your dog gets the best possible care.

### Contact Your Veterinarian Immediately

As soon as you suspect your dog has ingested cannabis, call your veterinarian or the nearest emergency veterinary clinic. Do not wait for symptoms to appear or worsen. Provide them with as much information as you can:

*   **What was ingested?** (e.g., plant material, edible, concentrate)
*   **How much?** (your best estimate)
*   **When was it ingested?** (approximate time)
*   **What are your dog's current symptoms?** (be specific)
*   **Your dog's breed, age, and weight.**

Be honest about the situation. Veterinarians are there to help your pet, not to judge you. The more information they have, the better they can assess the situation and recommend the appropriate course of action. This might involve bringing your dog in for an immediate examination.

### Do Not Induce Vomiting Without Guidance

Inducing vomiting might seem like a logical first step to remove the toxin. However, it can be dangerous if done incorrectly or if your dog is already showing certain symptoms. For instance, if your dog is already lethargic, uncoordinated, or having seizures, inducing vomiting can lead to aspiration pneumonia, where vomit is inhaled into the lungs.

Your veterinarian will advise you if inducing vomiting is safe and appropriate, often within a short window after ingestion (usually within 30-60 minutes). They might ask you to bring your dog to the clinic so they can induce vomiting safely and monitor your dog afterwards. Never administer hydrogen peroxide or other substances without explicit veterinary instruction. This is why contacting them first is paramount.

### Secure the Area and Remaining Substance

While you are contacting the vet or preparing to leave, ensure no other pets or children can access the remaining cannabis. Clean up any remnants immediately and secure your stash. This prevents further accidental ingestions. ## Veterinary Treatment for Cannabis Intoxication

When your dog arrives at the veterinary clinic after suspected cannabis ingestion, the veterinary team will focus on two main goals: decontamination and supportive care. The specific treatment plan will depend on the amount ingested, the time since ingestion, and the severity of your dog's symptoms. This is why bringing your dog in promptly is so important.

Veterinary professionals are equipped to handle these emergencies. They have the knowledge and tools to stabilize your pet and facilitate their recovery. Do not underestimate the value of professional medical intervention in these cases. Their actions can prevent the intoxication from worsening and ensure a quicker, safer recovery.

### Initial Assessment and Decontamination

Upon arrival, the vet will perform a thorough physical examination. They will check your dog's vital signs, including heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, and neurological status. They will also likely ask you to confirm details about the exposure. Diagnosis is often made based on history and clinical signs. Specific tests for THC are available but usually not necessary for immediate treatment.

If the ingestion was recent (typically within 30-60 minutes) and your dog is stable and alert, the vet may induce vomiting using medications. This helps remove unabsorbed cannabis from the stomach. After vomiting, or if vomiting is not induced, activated charcoal may be given. Activated charcoal helps bind to toxins in the digestive tract, preventing their absorption into the bloodstream and aiding in their elimination through feces. This is a common and effective decontamination method.

### Supportive Care and Monitoring

Since there is no specific antidote for THC, treatment primarily focuses on supportive care. This means managing the symptoms and ensuring your dog's comfort and safety until the THC is naturally metabolized and eliminated from their system. This supportive care might include:

*   **Intravenous (IV) Fluids:** To help maintain hydration, support kidney function, and facilitate the excretion of toxins.
*   **Temperature Regulation:** Dogs often experience hypothermia (low body temperature) with cannabis toxicity. The vet team will use warming blankets or other methods to maintain a normal body temperature.
*   **Sedation:** If your dog is extremely agitated, anxious, or experiencing severe tremors, the vet might administer sedatives to calm them and prevent self-injury.
*   **Anti-nausea Medication:** To control vomiting if it is persistent.
*   **Monitoring:** Continuous monitoring of vital signs, neurological status, and hydration is crucial. Dogs with severe intoxication might need to stay in the hospital for 24-72 hours or more, especially since the effects of ingested weed can last a long time. The recovery period can vary. Mild cases might resolve within a few hours. More severe intoxications can require hospitalization for several days until the dog is stable and alert again. ## Preventing Cannabis Exposure in Dogs

The best treatment for cannabis intoxication is prevention. As responsible pet owners, we must take proactive steps to ensure our dogs cannot access harmful substances. Dogs, by nature, are curious and often driven by their keen sense of smell. What might seem obvious to us as "off-limits" is simply an intriguing new smell to them.

Implementing strict prevention strategies creates a safer home environment for your pet. It eliminates the risk of accidental ingestion altogether. This vigilance is a cornerstone of responsible pet ownership, extending beyond just cannabis to all potential household toxins. My own experience has taught me that dogs are incredibly resourceful when it comes to finding things they shouldn't.

### Secure Storage Methods

The most effective prevention strategy is secure storage. All cannabis products—whether flower, edibles, concentrates, or tinctures—must be stored in a place inaccessible to pets. This means more than just putting it on a high shelf. Dogs are agile, and some can jump or climb surprisingly well.

Consider using:

*   **Locked Containers:** Child-proof or pet-proof containers are ideal. These can be hard plastic bins with secure latches or even small, lockable safes.
*   **High Cabinets or Drawers:** Store cannabis in cabinets or drawers that your dog cannot open or reach. Ensure these are securely closed.
*   **Out of Sight, Out of Mind:** Do not leave cannabis products on coffee tables, nightstands, or in open bags. A dog's nose will find it.
*   **Original Packaging:** Keep products in their original packaging, as this often has warnings and dose information if accidental ingestion occurs.

Remember, dogs are very motivated by food smells. Edibles, in particular, are extremely tempting. A brownie or gummy bear smells just like a treat to them. Be extra diligent with these products.

### Awareness and Communication with Guests

Educate everyone in your household and any guests about the importance of pet safety. If you have visitors who use cannabis, make them aware that pets are present and that all products must be securely stored. It is not uncommon for guests to inadvertently leave items within a dog's reach.

Clear communication can prevent many accidents. Politely ask guests to keep their personal items, including any cannabis products, in sealed containers in their bags or in a secure location away from pets. This goes for discarded joints or vape pens too—ensure they are disposed of safely in a sealed outdoor bin, not an accessible indoor trash can.

### Training and Supervision

While secure storage is paramount, training your dog can also add an extra layer of protection. Teach your dog the "leave it" command. This command can be invaluable if you drop something on the floor that your dog should not eat. Consistent training reinforces this behavior.

Supervision is also key, especially when cannabis is actively being used or is temporarily out of its secure storage. Never leave a dog unsupervised in a room where cannabis is accessible, even for a moment. Puppies and young dogs, being the most curious, require even more vigilant supervision. ## FAQ Section

### How long does it take for a dog to show symptoms after eating weed?

Symptoms typically appear within 30 minutes to a few hours after ingestion. The exact time depends on the form of cannabis (e.g., edibles take longer to digest), the amount consumed, and your dog's metabolism. It is important to monitor your dog closely in the hours following suspected ingestion.

### Is cannabis intoxication fatal for dogs?

While rare, severe cannabis intoxication can be fatal, especially with high doses of potent products or in very small, vulnerable dogs. Most dogs recover fully with prompt veterinary care. However, without treatment, severe symptoms like seizures, coma, or respiratory depression can become life-threatening.

### Can CBD products make my dog high?

No, well-sourced CBD products derived from hemp typically contain less than 0.3% THC and should not make your dog high. CBD (cannabidiol) is non-psychoactive. However, ensure any CBD product you use is tested by a third party for THC content and purity to avoid accidental intoxication.

### What is the difference between marijuana and hemp for dogs?

Marijuana contains high levels of THC, the psychoactive compound that causes a "high." Hemp, on the other hand, contains very low levels of THC (under 0.3%) and is primarily rich in CBD and other beneficial cannabinoids. This distinction is crucial for understanding the psychoactive risk to dogs.

### Should I induce vomiting if my dog eats weed?

You should never induce vomiting without explicit instruction from a veterinarian. If your dog is already lethargic, unstable, or having seizures, inducing vomiting can cause aspiration pneumonia, where vomit enters the lungs. Contact your vet immediately for guidance on whether or not it is safe to induce vomiting.

### Can dogs get high from secondhand weed smoke?

Yes, dogs can get high from secondhand weed smoke, though usually less severely than from direct ingestion. If a dog is in a poorly ventilated area with dense smoke, they can inhale enough THC to show mild symptoms of intoxication, such as lethargy or disorientation. Always ensure proper ventilation around pets.
