## The White German Shepherd: A Historical Perspective

The story of what are all white German Shepherds called begins with the original German Shepherd Dog. Early German Shepherds came in many colors, including white. The breed's founder, Captain Max von Stephanitz, even owned a white German Shepherd. This dog was named Greif. Greif appeared in the early lineage of the German Shepherd breed.

White was never a sign of albinism or a fault in early German Shepherds. It was a natural coat color, like black or sable. The white coat comes from a recessive gene. This means both parent dogs must carry the gene for a white puppy to be born. These white dogs were part of the breed's initial development.

Over time, breed standards changed. In 1933, the German Shepherd Dog Club of Germany updated its standard. They declared white a disqualifying color. This decision aimed to standardize the breed's appearance. It also sought to prevent potential misinterpretations about the white coat. Many believed it indicated albinism or health issues, even though it did not.

Despite this decision, white-coated German Shepherds still existed. Breeders outside Germany continued to value them. Many white German Shepherds found their way to the United States and Canada. These dogs carried the white gene forward. They maintained the original German Shepherd characteristics. This split in recognition marked a significant point in their history.

## From Disqualification to Distinct Breed Status

The exclusion of white coats from the German Shepherd standard led to a divergence. Breeders in North America and Europe continued to breed white German Shepherds. They focused on preserving the breed's original temperament and structure. They simply accepted the white coat color. These dogs proved to be intelligent, loyal, and capable working animals.

As their numbers grew, supporters sought official recognition. They argued that these dogs were healthy and genetically sound. They felt the white coat was merely a cosmetic difference. These efforts gained traction, particularly in Switzerland. Swiss enthusiasts played a vital role in establishing a separate breed.

In the early 1990s, the Federation Cynologique Internationale (FCI) recognized the White Swiss Shepherd Dog. This was a major step. The FCI is a global canine organization. Its recognition meant the White Swiss Shepherd became a distinct breed internationally. This new breed was called "Berger Blanc Suisse" in French-speaking regions. It solidified their identity apart from the traditional German Shepherd.

This recognition solved the identity crisis for white German Shepherds. It gave them a specific name and a defined breed standard. Now, if you ask "what are all white German Shepherds called" in many parts of the world, the answer is often "White Swiss Shepherd." This breed is now carefully managed. Breeders focus on health, temperament, and appearance, just like any other purebred dog.

This decision came from historical concerns, as mentioned before. People worried about health issues, despite no scientific basis for such fears related to the white coat itself.

In North America, many white-coated German Shepherds continue to be bred. They are still registered as German Shepherds, but often without AKC papers for showing purposes. Some breeders may sell them as "White German Shepherds."

This difference in recognition affects many things. It impacts how breeders register dogs. It also influences how dog enthusiasts view these animals. ### Common Health Concerns

*   **Hip and Elbow Dysplasia:** This is a common orthopedic issue in large breeds. It involves malformation of the hip or elbow joints. Good breeders perform health clearances.

## FAQ Section

### Q1: Is a white German Shepherd rare?
White German Shepherds are not rare in terms of their genetic occurrence. The white coat is a recessive trait, meaning both parents must carry the gene for a white puppy to be born. They are less common than standard-colored German Shepherds due to historical breeding preferences and breed standard exclusions by some kennel clubs, which limited their proliferation in official lines for a time.

### Q2: Do white German Shepherds have different health problems than regular German Shepherds?
No, the white coat color itself does not cause unique health problems. The common health issues seen in white German Shepherds are the same orthopedic and genetic conditions found in standard-colored German Shepherds, such as hip and elbow dysplasia. Misconceptions about white coats indicating albinism or increased health risks are unfounded. Responsible breeders screen for health.

### Q3: What is the temperament of a White Swiss Shepherd like?
White Swiss Shepherds generally share the intelligence, loyalty, and protective instincts of German Shepherds. Many lines are described as having a slightly softer, more approachable temperament compared to some high-drive working line German Shepherds. They are excellent family dogs, highly trainable, and devoted companions who thrive with proper socialization and training.

### Q4: Can White German Shepherds be registered with the AKC?
White German Shepherds with a pure white coat cannot be registered for conformation showing with the American Kennel Club (AKC) because white is a disqualifying fault in their German Shepherd breed standard. However, if their parents are AKC-registered German Shepherds, the puppies themselves might be registered, though they cannot compete in shows.

### Q5: How much do White German Shepherds typically cost?
The cost of a White German Shepherd or White Swiss Shepherd puppy can vary widely. Factors include the breeder's reputation, the puppy's lineage, health clearances, and location. Prices generally range from $1,500 to $4,000 or more from reputable breeders who prioritize health testing and responsible breeding practices.

### Q6: What's the difference between a white German Shepherd and an albino dog?
A white German Shepherd is not an albino. Albinism is a genetic condition resulting in a complete lack of pigment, characterized by pink eyes, pink noses, and pale skin. White German Shepherds, including White Swiss Shepherds, have blue or brown eyes, black noses, and black paw pads, indicating they possess pigment despite their white coat.
