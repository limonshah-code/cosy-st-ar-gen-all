### Genetics of Dog Eye Color: Understanding Melanin

Dog eye color results from the amount and type of melanin pigment in the iris. Melanin is a dark brown or black pigment. Different concentrations and distributions of melanin create various eye colors. Eumelanin is the most common type. The more eumelanin present, the darker the eye color will be. This explains why most dogs have brown eyes.

Genes control melanin production and distribution. A dog inherits specific genes from its parents. These genes determine the amount of pigment in the iris. For instance, a high concentration of eumelanin leads to dark brown eyes. A lower concentration can result in lighter brown or amber eyes. Genetic mutations can cause other colors.

Green eyes in dogs are very rare. They happen when there is a very low amount of melanin mixed with a yellow pigment. This combination creates the green hue. However, specific genes for this combination are not present in most dog breeds. This is particularly true for breeds like the Alaskan Malamute, which have strong genetic predispositions for brown eyes. This genetic makeup ensures that brown eyes remain the standard.

#### The Role of Genes in Eye Pigmentation

Several genes contribute to a dog's eye color. The Merle gene, for example, can cause blue eyes in some breeds, but it does not exist in Malamutes. Another gene, located on chromosome 10, influences eye color in various breeds. It controls the amount of melanin produced. Malamutes have genes that promote high melanin production in their eyes. This genetic blueprint locks in their brown eye color.

Breeding practices also play a crucial role. Responsible breeders select dogs with desired traits. They aim to produce puppies that meet the breed standard. This includes maintaining the dark brown eye color. This careful selection ensures genetic consistency over generations. It reinforces the characteristic appearance of the Alaskan Malamute.

### Puppy Eye Color: A Temporary Greenish Hue?

Many puppies are born with blue or grey eyes. This is a common phenomenon across almost all dog breeds. Their eyes begin to produce melanin after birth. The true adult eye color develops over several weeks. For Alaskan Malamute puppies, this initial blue or grey color gradually changes to brown. This process usually takes place between six and sixteen weeks of age. During this transition period, a puppy's eyes might appear to have a greenish-blue tint.

This greenish hue is usually temporary. It is not an indication that the puppy will have green eyes as an adult. It simply represents an intermediate stage of melanin development. The pigments are still forming and settling into their final color. As more melanin is deposited into the iris, the eyes deepen to a rich brown. Observing this change is a fascinating part of watching puppies grow. My own Malamute puppy had eyes that seemed to shift colors for weeks.

It is important for owners not to mistake this temporary color for a permanent green. Reputable breeders can tell you what to expect. They understand the normal developmental stages of a Malamute puppy's eyes. If a puppy's eyes remain blue or a light color beyond the typical developmental window, it is not a standard Malamute. This is why it is essential to purchase from responsible breeders.

### Distinguishing True Green from Other Eye Colors

What people sometimes perceive as green in a dog's eyes can often be something else. Lighting conditions play a big role in how we see eye color. In certain lights, brown eyes might pick up environmental reflections. These reflections can give them a greenish cast. Amber eyes, which are a lighter shade of brown, can also sometimes be mistaken for green. However, amber eyes are a distinct color. They result from less melanin than dark brown eyes.

True green eyes are a very specific hue. They are much rarer in dogs than in humans or cats. If a dog truly has green eyes, it suggests either a mixed heritage or a rare genetic mutation. This mutation would be outside the typical genetic makeup of an Alaskan Malamute. An Alaskan Malamute with green eyes would almost certainly not be a purebred. It might have genes from another breed that carries such eye color.

It is helpful to know the difference. When evaluating a Malamute, always look for the standard dark brown eyes. My advice is always to check the dog in natural daylight. This avoids any misleading perceptions from artificial lighting. Understanding these distinctions helps maintain the integrity of the breed.

#### Amber Eyes vs. Green Eyes

Amber eyes are a possibility in some dog breeds, but not typically in purebred Malamutes. They are lighter than brown, appearing golden or yellowish-brown. This color occurs due to a reduced amount of eumelanin. Green eyes, by contrast, involve a specific blend of very low melanin and a yellow pigment. This distinct combination is generally not found in the Malamute genetic code. So, while some may confuse amber with a "light green," they are genetically distinct and neither is standard for Malamutes.
