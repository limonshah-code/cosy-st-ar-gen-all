The AKC standard clearly states the acceptable eye color for Alaskan Malamutes. Their eyes must be almond-shaped. The color should be brown. All shades of brown are accepted. ## The Genetics Behind Eye Color in Dogs

Eye color in dogs, including the Alaskan Malamute, is fascinating. It is all about genetics. Specific genes control the amount and type of pigment in the iris. The iris is the colored part of the eye. Melanin is the main pigment involved. More melanin typically means darker eyes. Less melanin leads to lighter eyes.

There are several genes that influence eye color in dogs. The Merle gene is one well-known example. This gene can dilute pigment. It creates patches of lighter color in the coat. It can also cause blue eyes. Breeds like the Australian Shepherd often carry the Merle gene. This is why many Australian Shepherds have blue eyes. You can learn more about this in articles like [Do All Australian Shepherds Have Blue Eyes](https://cosyhomecreation.com/do-all-australian-shepherds-have-blue-eyes) or [Why Do Some Aussies Have Blue Eyes](https://cosyhomecreation.com/why-do-some-aussies-have-blue-eyes). The Merle gene is not present in purebred Alaskan Malamutes.

Another gene, often called the "blue eye gene," can cause blue eyes in some breeds. This gene works differently than Merle. It is responsible for the striking blue eyes seen in Siberian Huskies. This gene essentially stops melanin from developing in the iris. This creates a blue appearance. Blue eyes result from light reflecting off unpigmented layers in the iris. It is similar to how the sky looks blue. Purebred Malamutes generally do not carry this specific blue eye gene.

Breeders use genetic knowledge to understand inherited traits. They carefully select breeding pairs. This helps ensure puppies meet breed standards. For Malamutes, this means consistently brown eyes. Understanding these genetic mechanisms is key. It explains why certain eye colors are common or rare in different breeds.

Sometimes, a puppy's eyes might appear blue at birth. All puppies are born with blue eyes. Their permanent eye color develops as they grow. Pigment production increases over time. The eyes usually change to their adult color around 8 to 16 weeks of age. If a Malamute puppy's eyes remain blue, it suggests a genetic factor not standard to the breed. ## Why Purebred Alaskan Malamutes Rarely Have Blue Eyes

The primary reason purebred Alaskan Malamutes rarely have blue eyes is their strict genetic makeup. The breed standard, upheld by kennel clubs like the AKC, specifies brown eyes as the only acceptable color. This standard reflects generations of selective breeding. Breeders chose dogs with brown eyes to continue the Malamute lineage. This process ensured the gene for blue eyes was largely absent from the purebred population.

This absence directly contrasts with other Northern breeds, especially Siberian Huskies. Huskies frequently display brilliant blue eyes, or even heterochromia (one blue eye and one brown eye). This difference in eye color is a key distinguishing feature between the two breeds. Although they share a similar appearance and origin, their genetics for eye color are distinct. People often confuse the two breeds because of their wolf-like appearance. However, their eye color helps tell them apart. If a purebred Malamute puppy develops blue eyes, it is considered an anomaly. It is a deviation from the established breed type. This usually means one of two things. First, the puppy might not be a purebred Malamute. It could be a mix, even if it looks like a Malamute. The blue eye gene likely came from a different breed. Second, there could be a very rare genetic mutation. However, such mutations are uncommon. They would still lead to a dog not meeting the breed standard.

Responsible breeders prioritize maintaining the integrity of the breed. They adhere to the breed standard. They also ensure the health and temperament of their dogs. They do not breed for "rare" blue eyes in Malamutes. Doing so would go against established guidelines. It could also potentially introduce unknown genetic factors. For me, seeing a true purebred Malamute always means seeing those rich, brown eyes. It is a sign of their heritage.

Understanding this genetic distinction is vital. It helps current and potential owners appreciate the unique traits of the Alaskan Malamute. It also helps prevent misunderstandings about what constitutes a true purebred. The brown eyes are not just a preference; they are a defining characteristic.

## Can Mixed-Breed Malamutes Have Blue Eyes?

Yes, mixed-breed Malamutes can definitely have blue eyes. This happens when an Alaskan Malamute is bred with another dog breed that carries the gene for blue eyes. The offspring then inherit the blue eye trait from the non-Malamute parent. This is the most common way to see a Malamute with blue eyes. It is important to remember these are not purebred Malamutes.

Consider crosses with breeds like the Siberian Husky. Huskies are famous for their blue eyes. A "Malamute-Husky mix," sometimes called an "Alusky," can easily inherit blue eyes. The Husky parent passes on the necessary genetic information for blue eye color. This results in stunning blue-eyed puppies. Similarly, if a Malamute is crossed with an Australian Shepherd, blue eyes are also possible. Australian Shepherds often have blue eyes. The merle gene in Aussies can contribute to this.

Other breeds known to carry the blue eye gene include Border Collies, some types of Dalmatians, and certain French Bulldogs, though the latter might be less common crosses. ## Are Blue Eyes a Health Concern for Alaskan Malamutes?

The presence of blue eyes in an Alaskan Malamute, while not typical for the purebred standard, does not automatically signify a health concern. For breeds where blue eyes are a standard trait, like the Siberian Husky, blue eyes are simply a genetic variation. They are not linked to poor health. For example, a Husky with blue eyes is perfectly healthy in that regard. Similarly, a Malamute mix with blue eyes due to its heritage from a blue-eyed breed typically faces no extra health risks solely because of its eye color.

However, there are nuances to consider. Some types of blue eyes are associated with specific genetic conditions in certain breeds. For instance, the Merle gene, which can cause blue eyes in breeds like Australian Shepherds, can also be linked to hearing and vision problems if two Merle-carrying dogs are bred together (double Merle). This is a distinct genetic pathway from the non-Merle blue eye gene found in Huskies. Since purebred Malamutes do not carry the Merle gene, this specific risk usually does not apply directly to them.

For a purebred Malamute that exhibits blue eyes, it is most likely due to either mixed ancestry or a very rare, spontaneous mutation. If it's a mix, the health implications would depend on the health predispositions of the other breed involved. If it's a rare mutation in a seemingly purebred line, it might warrant a closer look at the dog's overall health and parentage. However, the blue eye color itself is not usually the cause of health problems. The color is a result, not a cause.

In general, the health of a dog with blue eyes is similar to that of a dog with brown eyes. Factors like breeding practices, nutrition, and overall genetic health of the parents are far more significant. For example, questions like [Do Dogs With Blue Eyes Have More Problems](https://cosyhomecreation.com/do-dogs-with-blue-eyes-have-more-problems) are usually answered with "no" in the vast majority of cases. Any dog can develop eye conditions regardless of eye color. These include cataracts or progressive retinal atrophy. These conditions are not unique to blue-eyed dogs.

My personal philosophy is to focus on the dog's overall well-being. This includes their temperament, energy levels, and general physical condition. Eye color is a cosmetic trait. While it matters for breed standards, it rarely impacts a dog's health directly. Always choose a dog from a reputable breeder or rescue. This ensures they prioritize health screenings and good genetics.

## FAQ Section

### Q1: Is it normal for an Alaskan Malamute to have blue eyes?

No, it is not normal for a purebred Alaskan Malamute to have blue eyes. The breed standard, as recognized by major kennel clubs, specifies that all shades of brown are the only acceptable eye colors. Blue eyes are considered a disqualifying fault in purebred Malamutes and indicate a deviation from the established breed type.

### Q2: Do blue eyes in a Malamute indicate a health issue?

Generally, blue eyes in a Malamute do not indicate a health issue on their own. If a Malamute has blue eyes, it is almost certainly a mixed breed. The blue eye gene would come from the other parent breed. Health concerns are usually tied to specific genetic conditions, like those linked to the Merle gene in some breeds, which purebred Malamutes do not carry.

### Q3: Are blue-eyed Malamutes purebred?

No, blue-eyed Malamutes are typically not purebred. If an Alaskan Malamute has blue eyes, it means it likely has mixed ancestry. The blue eye trait comes from another breed, like a Siberian Husky, which carries the gene for blue eyes. A purebred Alaskan Malamute will always have brown eyes according to the breed standard.

### Q4: How can I tell if my Malamute puppy will have blue eyes?

All puppies are born with blue eyes. Their true adult eye color develops as they mature. For a Malamute puppy, its eyes should begin to change to brown between 8 and 16 weeks of age. If your Malamute puppy's eyes remain blue past this period, it suggests they may be a mixed breed.

### Q5: What eye colors are accepted for Alaskan Malamutes?

The only accepted eye color for a purebred Alaskan Malamute is brown. This can include various shades, from light amber to very dark brown. This standard helps define the breed's distinct appearance and distinguishes it from other Northern breeds that may have blue eyes.

### Q6: Can a Malamute have one blue eye and one brown eye?

A purebred Alaskan Malamute cannot have one blue eye and one brown eye (a condition called heterochromia). Heterochromia, or "parti-colored" eyes, is typically seen in breeds like Siberian Huskies or Australian Shepherds, where the blue eye gene is present. If a Malamute has heterochromia, it indicates mixed parentage.
