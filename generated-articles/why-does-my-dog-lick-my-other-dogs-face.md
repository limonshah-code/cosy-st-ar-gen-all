Understanding **why does my dog lick my other dogs face** provides valuable insight into their social world.

They use licking to build and maintain their bonds.

## Submissive Licking: Showing Respect

Licking another dog's face can also be a clear sign of submission. A dog lower in the social hierarchy may lick the face of a higher-ranking dog. This behavior communicates respect and acknowledges the other dog's dominance. It is a way for dogs to prevent conflict. This avoids direct confrontations.

Submissive licking often involves the licker making itself smaller. It may crouch or lower its head. The dog might also avert its gaze slightly. These body language signals accompany the licking. They reinforce the message of deference. This is a common way for dogs to show that they are not a threat.

This behavior stems from puppyhood. Puppies lick their mother's face to show they are harmless. They also do it to solicit care and food. As dogs grow, this appeasement gesture continues. It helps maintain peace within the pack structure. This type of licking helps to keep social order.

If you observe your dogs, you might see this dynamic. The dog doing the licking might seem a bit more tentative. The dog being licked might stand tall or ignore the licker at first. This shows the accepted social roles. This interaction is a normal part of canine group dynamics. It confirms who holds authority.

Understanding submissive licking helps us interpret dog interactions better. It shows that licking is not always just about affection. It can also be about social structure. This behavior helps dogs live together peacefully. It allows them to respect each other's boundaries. ## Resource Solicitation: A Vestige of Puppyhood

Dogs licking faces can also be a plea for resources. This behavior has roots in puppyhood. When puppies are young, they lick their mother's muzzle. This action encourages the mother dog to regurgitate food for them. It is an instinctual way to get nourishment. This behavior is deeply ingrained in canine development.

Even adult dogs sometimes use this same action. They might lick another dog's face to ask for something. This "something" could be food, attention, or access to a toy. It is a learned behavior from their early days. It worked for them as puppies, so they try it as adults. They hope it will yield a positive result.

The dog doing the licking may appear eager or slightly persistent. They might nudge the other dog with their nose. Their tail might wag, showing excitement. The other dog might respond by sharing a toy or moving away. This interaction shows a clear request. It highlights the licker's desire for a specific item.

For example, my smaller dog sometimes licks the face of my larger dog. She does this when the larger dog has a favored chew toy. It is her subtle way of asking, "Can I have a turn?" This behavior is often successful. The larger dog sometimes drops the toy after these gentle requests. This shows the power of this learned solicitation.

This type of licking demonstrates how early instincts carry into adult life. It is not always about hunger for food. It can be for any desired resource. It shows how dogs adapt their communication. They use what worked in the past. It is a clever strategy to get what they want.

## Stress, Anxiety, and Calming Signals

Sometimes, a dog llicks another dog's face as a calming signal. This behavior might occur when one dog feels stressed or anxious. The licking dog tries to diffuse tension in the environment. It is a way to signal "I mean no harm" or "Let's calm down." This helps maintain peace in the group.

Calming signals are behaviors dogs use to communicate their intentions. They show a desire to avoid conflict. They can also show a wish to de-escalate a tense situation. Licking is one such signal. It aims to reduce arousal in the other dog. It helps them both relax.

You might observe this behavior in specific situations. For example, if two dogs are getting too excited during playtime, one might start licking the other's face. This can be a gentle way to say, "This is getting too intense, let's cool down." The licking dog seeks to restore balance. It aims for a calmer interaction.

Another scenario involves a dog feeling overwhelmed. If a new dog enters the household, an existing dog might lick its face. This shows a desire for peaceful introduction. It helps ease tension for both animals. The licking dog tries to make the situation less threatening. This behavior is a form of reassurance.

This type of licking can also be a displacement behavior. If a dog feels internal conflict, it might lick. This helps them cope with their own stress. The target of the licking is simply available. The act of licking helps them self-soothe. It can show that a dog is uncomfortable. Recognizing this helps us understand their emotional state.

## When Licking Becomes a Concern: Observing Canine Health

Most of the time, a dog licking another dog's face is a normal and healthy behavior. However, there are instances when you should pay closer attention. Excessive or obsessive licking might indicate an underlying issue. It could be a sign of stress, discomfort, or even a medical problem. Observing changes in this behavior is important.

If one dog suddenly starts licking another dog excessively, investigate. Does the licker seem unusually anxious? Is the dog being licked showing signs of discomfort? Sometimes, a dog will persistently lick an area on another dog that is sore or injured. Their keen sense of smell can detect issues. They might be trying to "clean" a wound or provide comfort.

Observe the licked dog's reaction. Do they tolerate the licking or try to move away? Constant avoidance by the licked dog might signal irritation. It could also mean the licking dog is being overly persistent. An otherwise calm dog suddenly showing irritation is a red flag. This indicates the interaction is no longer positive.

Consider the health of the licked dog's face. Is there any redness, swelling, or hair loss where the licking occurs? These physical signs might point to a skin irritation or infection. The licking could be making the problem worse. It is vital to check for any visible issues.

Excessive licking can also be a sign of canine compulsive disorder. This is rare in inter-dog licking. However, if the behavior seems relentless and serves no clear social purpose, consult a vet. A vet can rule out medical causes for the licking. They can also provide guidance on behavioral interventions. Normal licking is a balanced, reciprocal act. Any shift in this balance deserves a closer look.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it normal for dogs to lick each other's faces often?

Yes, it is very normal for dogs to lick each other's faces often. This behavior serves many purposes in canine communication. Dogs use face licking for greetings, showing affection, and social bonding. It helps them maintain a healthy social dynamic within their group. Regular face licking is a positive sign of a good relationship between dogs.

### Does face licking show one dog is dominant over another?

Face licking can sometimes show submission, not dominance. A dog lower in the social hierarchy might lick the face of a higher-ranking dog. This signals respect and helps prevent conflict. While dominant dogs might accept these licks, the act of licking itself is usually a submissive gesture. It is a way to show deference and maintain peace.

### Can excessive face licking indicate a health problem?

In most cases, no. However, excessive or sudden changes in face licking can sometimes point to a health problem. A dog might lick a specific spot if the other dog has an injury or skin irritation. Obsessive licking without clear social reasons could also suggest anxiety. If you notice any unusual changes, consult your veterinarian.

### Why do puppies lick their mother's face?

Puppies lick their mother's face primarily to stimulate regurgitation of food. This is an instinctual behavior vital for their early feeding. As they grow, this behavior adapts. It becomes a general appeasement gesture. It helps them show submission and solicit attention or resources from other adult dogs.

### Should I stop my dogs from licking each other's faces?

Generally, no, you should not stop your dogs from licking each other's faces. This is a natural and healthy part of canine communication. It helps them bond, express affection, and maintain social order. Only intervene if the licking becomes excessive, obsessive, or if one dog consistently shows discomfort or irritation.
