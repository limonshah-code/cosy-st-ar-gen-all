## How To Clean Cleats Smell: Eliminating Odor Effectively

There is nothing quite like the unmistakable smell of well-worn athletic cleats. After countless games and practices, those cleats can develop a powerful odor. This scent is often a mix of sweat, dirt, and bacteria, making them less than pleasant to have around.

I know how frustrating it is to deal with smelly sports gear. You want to keep your equipment in good shape, but the smell can be a real challenge. This comprehensive guide will walk you through everything you need to know. We will cover why cleats smell and effective methods to tackle those stubborn odors. You will also learn how to prevent bad smells from returning, keeping your cleats fresh for every game.

### Takeaway

*   **Address odor source:** Clean cleats thoroughly to remove bacteria and sweat.
*   **Use deodorizers:** Apply baking soda, white vinegar, or commercial products.
*   **Ensure proper drying:** Air dry completely after each use to prevent moisture buildup.
*   **Maintain regularly:** Practice consistent cleaning and storage habits.

### Clear, Concise Answer to How To Clean Cleats Smell

To clean cleats smell, you must first remove any dirt and debris. Then, wash them with soap and water or use a gentle machine cycle. Follow this with a deodorizing treatment like baking soda or white vinegar. Finally, dry them thoroughly in a well-ventilated area, avoiding direct heat.

## Understanding Cleat Odor: What Causes That Lingering Smell?

The distinctive smell coming from your cleats is not just dirt. It results from a combination of factors that create a perfect breeding ground for odor-causing agents. Understanding these causes helps you tackle the problem more effectively. I always start by knowing the enemy before I fight it.

Every time you wear your cleats, your feet sweat. This sweat contains moisture and nutrients that bacteria love. The inside of a cleat is often dark, warm, and damp. These conditions are ideal for bacteria to multiply rapidly. As bacteria grow, they break down sweat compounds. This process releases foul-smelling chemicals, creating the strong odor you associate with dirty cleats.

### The Role of Bacteria and Moisture

Bacteria are the primary culprits behind cleat odor. Your feet have millions of sweat glands. When you play sports, these glands produce a lot of moisture. This moisture gets trapped inside your cleats. It mixes with dead skin cells and other organic matter. This creates a nutrient-rich environment for bacteria to thrive.

The fabric and materials inside your cleats absorb this moisture. This keeps the environment damp, further encouraging bacterial growth. Without proper cleaning and drying, bacteria continue to multiply. This leads to an increasingly strong and unpleasant smell. Think of it like a tiny, odor-producing factory right on your feet.

### Sweat and Material Absorption

Different cleat materials absorb sweat differently. Synthetic materials might trap moisture more readily. Natural leather can absorb and hold onto moisture, making it harder to dry completely. Regardless of the material, sweat permeates the linings, insoles, and seams. This absorption creates persistent odor problems.

Over time, these materials become saturated with sweat and bacteria. Regular wear compounds the issue. This makes the odor difficult to remove with simple airing out. It often requires a deeper cleaning strategy. ## Essential Pre-Cleaning Steps for Smelly Cleats

Before you dive into deep cleaning, proper preparation is key. Skipping these initial steps can make the cleaning process less effective. It can also spread dirt and grime. I always take a few minutes for these preparatory tasks. They save a lot of effort later on.

First, take your cleats outside or find a well-ventilated area. This helps manage the initial strong smell. It also keeps dirt out of your living space. Prepare your tools for scrubbing away loose debris. This makes the deep cleaning much easier.

### Removing Loose Dirt and Debris

Begin by banging your cleats together to dislodge large clumps of dirt, mud, and grass. Do this over a trash can or outside. You do not want this mess inside your home. Next, use a stiff brush, an old toothbrush, or a dull knife to scrape off any remaining caked-on mud. Pay special attention to the studs and grooves. Dirt often gets trapped in these areas.

You can also use a small stick or a designated cleat brush for this task. Ensure you get into all the crevices. This step is crucial because it removes the bulk of the external grime. This prevents it from turning into muddy water during washing. I find this quick scrape-down makes a huge difference.

### Preparing for a Deep Clean

Once the loose dirt is gone, remove the laces and insoles from your cleats. These parts often hold a lot of odor and need separate attention. The insoles, especially, absorb a significant amount of sweat and bacteria. They benefit from a dedicated cleaning or even replacement. Many people overlook cleaning insoles. I make sure to clean them every time.

You can wash laces separately in a mesh bag or by hand. The insoles can be scrubbed with soap and water. You may also soak them in a deodorizing solution. Now your cleats are ready for a more thorough wash. ## Deep Cleaning Methods to Remove Cleat Smell

Once you have prepped your cleats, it is time for the main event: deep cleaning. There are a few effective ways to wash your cleats. The best method depends on the material of your cleats and how dirty they are. I have tried several approaches over the years. I find these two methods most reliable.

Remember, the goal is to eliminate the bacteria causing the smell. This requires more than just rinsing. It means a thorough wash that gets deep into the fabric and seams. Make sure you use appropriate cleaning agents to fight odor.

### The Washing Machine Method (Use Caution)

Washing cleats in a machine can be very effective, but it requires careful steps. First, place your cleats in a mesh laundry bag. This protects them and your washing machine from damage. You can also add some old towels to the load. These towels help balance the machine and cushion the cleats. Use a gentle cycle with cold water. Hot water can damage cleat materials, especially adhesives.

Add a small amount of mild detergent. Avoid harsh chemicals or bleach, as they can discolor or break down materials. After the wash cycle, *do not* put cleats in the dryer. The high heat will melt adhesives and shrink materials. Always air dry them, as I will explain later. This method is quick and gets deep into the fabric. However, it is not suitable for all cleat types, especially those made of delicate leather or with complex electronic components.

### Hand Washing with Soap and Water

Hand washing offers more control and is safer for delicate cleats. It is my preferred method for cleats that need special care. Start by filling a basin or bucket with lukewarm water. Add a few drops of mild dish soap or laundry detergent. Submerge your cleats, or at least the foot area, in the soapy water.

Use a soft brush or sponge to scrub the entire surface. Pay close attention to the inside lining, the tongue, and any soiled areas. For stubborn stains, you can create a paste of baking soda and a little water. Apply it to the stain and scrub gently. Rinse the cleats thoroughly under running water until all soap residue is gone. Squeeze out excess water. Then prepare them for drying. ## Natural Deodorizers and Odor Eliminators for Cleats

Once your cleats are clean, the next step is to tackle any remaining odor. Even after washing, some lingering smells can persist. This happens especially with deeply embedded bacteria. Natural deodorizers are fantastic for this task. They are safe, effective, and often already in your home. I rely on these natural solutions frequently.

These methods work by absorbing odors, neutralizing bacteria, or providing a fresh scent. They are an eco-friendly alternative to chemical sprays. They also prevent new odors from forming in clean cleats.

### Baking Soda Power

Baking soda is a miracle worker for odors. It is a natural desiccant and odor absorber. After your cleats are dry, sprinkle a generous amount of baking soda inside each cleat. Make sure it reaches the toe area and covers the insole. You can use an old sock filled with baking soda, tie it off, and place it inside the cleat. This prevents a powdery mess.

Leave the baking soda in for at least 24 hours, or even longer for very strong odors. The longer it sits, the more odor it absorbs. Baking soda neutralizes acidic and basic odor molecules. This makes it incredibly effective. Simply tap out the baking soda afterward. Your cleats will smell noticeably fresher. I often do this overnight after a big game.

### White Vinegar Solutions

White vinegar is another powerful natural deodorizer and disinfectant. Its acetic acid kills bacteria and fungi that cause odors. Create a solution of equal parts white vinegar and water in a spray bottle. Lightly mist the inside of your cleats. Do not saturate them, as too much moisture can cause issues.

You can also soak your insoles in this solution for about 30 minutes. Rinse them thoroughly afterward. The vinegar smell will dissipate as the cleats dry. It leaves behind a clean, fresh scent. I find this method excellent for tackling deep-seated bacterial smells. Be careful with leather cleats, as vinegar can dry them out if used too often.

### Essential Oils and Citrus Peels

For a pleasant scent boost, essential oils can be a great addition. After cleaning and deodorizing, place a few drops of tea tree oil, lavender oil, or lemon oil on a cotton ball. Put the cotton ball inside each cleat overnight. Tea tree oil also has antibacterial properties. Alternatively, fresh citrus peels (lemon, orange, grapefruit) can absorb odors and leave a refreshing aroma.

Place a few peels inside your cleats overnight. The natural oils in the citrus peels fight the bad smells. Just remember to remove them in the morning before wearing your cleats. These methods are excellent for adding a final touch of freshness. ## Advanced Techniques for Stubborn Cleat Odors

Sometimes, basic cleaning and natural deodorizers are not enough. Some cleats develop extremely stubborn odors. These smells might require more targeted or advanced methods. If you have tried everything and the smell persists, do not despair. There are still options available. I keep a few advanced tricks up my sleeve for the toughest cases.

These techniques go beyond everyday cleaning. They aim to sanitize and deeply refresh the material. They often involve specialized products or conditions. These methods can bring even the most offensive cleats back to life.

### Specialized Shoe Deodorizers

Many commercial products are specifically designed to eliminate shoe odors. These often come in spray or powder form. They contain powerful odor-absorbing ingredients or antimicrobial agents. Look for sprays that boast enzymatic action. Enzymes break down the odor-causing bacteria and their byproducts. Sprays are easy to apply directly inside the cleats.

Powders, similar to baking soda, absorb moisture and neutralize smells. You sprinkle them in and shake them out. Some products also offer a continuous release of fragrance. Always follow the product instructions carefully. Ensure the product is safe for your cleat material. I often use these for a quick refresh between deep cleans.

### UV Shoe Sanitizers

For a high-tech solution, consider a UV shoe sanitizer. These devices use ultraviolet light to kill bacteria, viruses, and fungi. You place the device inside your cleats, and it emits UV-C light. This light disrupts the DNA of microorganisms, preventing them from reproducing. UV sanitizers are very effective at killing the source of the odor. They do not rely on chemicals or fragrances.

Most devices have a timer, and a typical cycle lasts 10-20 minutes. This method is great for consistent sanitization. It helps maintain freshness over time. It is an investment, but it keeps your cleats truly clean from harmful microbes. I find them very useful for long-term hygiene.

### Freezing for Odor Control

Freezing can be an unconventional but effective method for odor control. The extreme cold temperatures kill many of the odor-causing bacteria. Place your clean, dry cleats in a sealed plastic bag. This prevents moisture from getting in and protects your freezer contents. Then, put the bagged cleats in the freezer overnight.

The low temperature essentially puts the bacteria into suspended animation or kills them. Remove the cleats in the morning and let them thaw completely. Any remaining smell should be significantly reduced. This method is best used as a supplemental treatment after thorough cleaning. It is particularly good for very tough, embedded odors. ## Proper Drying and Storage to Prevent Future Smells

Cleaning your cleats is only half the battle. How you dry and store them is just as important. Improper drying and storage are major contributors to recurring odors. Moisture is the enemy when it comes to cleat smell. Without proper drying, bacteria quickly return, and the cycle repeats. I learned this the hard way early in my sports career.

Establishing a good post-game routine is crucial. This will keep your cleats fresh and extend their lifespan. Always prioritize drying and air circulation. This fights moisture and airborne spores.

### The Importance of Air Drying

After cleaning, thorough air drying is non-negotiable. Never put your cleats in a clothes dryer. High heat can damage the materials, adhesives, and overall structure of the cleats. Instead, find a well-ventilated area. This could be outdoors in the shade or indoors near a fan.

Remove the insoles and laces. This allows air to circulate freely inside the cleat. You can stuff your cleats with newspaper or paper towels. These materials absorb residual moisture. Change the paper every few hours until the cleats are completely dry. This process can take 24-48 hours. Patience is key here. Ensure they are bone dry before wearing them again or storing them away.

### Avoiding Direct Heat

While air drying is essential, direct heat sources should be avoided. Placing cleats in direct sunlight, on a radiator, or with a hairdryer can cause damage. The heat can cause materials to crack, shrink, or warp. It can also weaken the glue holding the cleat together. This leads to premature wear and tear.

Even if cleats feel dry on the outside, moisture can still lurk deep within the padding. Always opt for a gentle, consistent airflow. This ensures even drying without risking damage. Protecting the integrity of the materials helps them last longer. It also prevents the cleat from becoming a moist haven for bacteria.

### Smart Storage Solutions

Once your cleats are completely dry, store them properly. Do not just toss them into a dark, damp gym bag. This is a sure way for odors to return. Use a breathable shoe bag or leave them in the open air. A mesh bag or a shoe rack in a well-ventilated closet is ideal. This allows continuous airflow.

You can also place a few cedar chips or silica gel packets inside them. These absorb any residual moisture and impart a fresh scent. For longer-term storage, ensure they are in a cool, dry place. Avoiding damp basements or garages is a good idea. By storing them correctly, you maintain the freshness you worked hard to achieve. This also helps other footwear. ## Maintaining Freshness: Everyday Cleat Care Tips

Preventing odors is far easier than eliminating them. Incorporating simple habits into your routine can keep your cleats smelling fresh. This proactive approach saves you time and effort in the long run. I have found that consistency is the most important factor in cleat hygiene.

These daily tips are easy to implement. They create an environment where odor-causing bacteria cannot thrive. A little effort after each use goes a long way. This ensures your cleats are always ready for the next game.

### Regular Insole Replacement

Insoles bear the brunt of sweat and bacteria. They are often the primary source of persistent cleat odor. Even with regular cleaning, insoles can reach a point where they are beyond redemption. Consider replacing your insoles every few months, especially if you play frequently.

You can buy odor-absorbing insoles specifically designed to combat smell. These often contain activated charcoal or other antimicrobial agents. Replacing insoles is a quick and effective way to refresh your cleats. It provides a new barrier against moisture and bacteria. This simple step can dramatically improve overall cleat freshness.

### Post-Game Routines

Develop a consistent post-game routine for your cleats. As soon as you finish playing, take them off. Do not leave them stuffed in a gym bag for hours. This traps moisture and heat, creating a perfect bacterial incubator. Remove the insoles and loosen the laces.

Wipe down the outside with a damp cloth to remove surface dirt. Then, place them in a well-ventilated area to air dry immediately. A designated spot on a porch or near a fan works well. You can also sprinkle a little baking soda inside them after each use. This absorbs moisture and odors before they set in. This small habit prevents odors from building up.

### Rotating Cleats

If you play sports regularly, consider having two pairs of cleats. Rotating between pairs allows each pair to dry completely between uses. This is a game-changer for odor prevention. When one pair is resting, it has ample time to air out and dehumidify. This prevents the constant dampness that fuels bacterial growth.

This practice not only keeps your cleats fresher but also extends their lifespan. Constant moisture and bacteria degrade materials faster. By rotating, you reduce wear and tear on each pair. This is a smart investment for serious athletes. It ensures you always have a fresh, dry pair ready to go.

## FAQ Section

### Q1: Can I put my cleats in the washing machine?

A1: Yes, you can machine wash most synthetic cleats. Place them in a mesh laundry bag with some old towels to protect them and the machine. Use a gentle cycle with cold water and a mild detergent. Avoid hot water, as it can damage materials and adhesives. Always air dry completely afterward.

### Q2: How often should I clean my cleats to prevent smell?

A2: You should air dry your cleats immediately after every use. For a deeper clean, aim to wash them thoroughly every 2-4 weeks, depending on how often you play and how much they smell. Regular spot cleaning and deodorizing with baking soda can help maintain freshness between washes.

### Q3: What is the best natural product to remove cleat smell?

A3: Baking soda is the most effective natural product for removing cleat smell. It absorbs moisture and neutralizes odors. Sprinkle a generous amount inside dry cleats and leave it overnight. White vinegar is also excellent for its antibacterial properties; use a diluted spray solution.

### Q4: Will freezing my cleats kill the odor-causing bacteria?

A4: Freezing can help kill some odor-causing bacteria by exposing them to extreme cold. Place your clean, dry cleats in a sealed plastic bag and leave them in the freezer overnight. This method is effective for reducing stubborn odors, but it should be used in conjunction with regular cleaning.

### Q5: Can I use fabric softener or bleach on my cleats?

A5: No, avoid using fabric softener or bleach on your cleats. Fabric softener can leave a residue that traps dirt and can be difficult to rinse out. Bleach can damage cleat materials, especially colored fabrics, and weaken adhesives. Stick to mild detergents and natural deodorizers.

### Q6: How can I prevent my new cleats from developing a smell?

A6: To prevent new cleats from smelling, always air dry them thoroughly after each use. Remove insoles to aid drying. Consider using moisture-absorbing inserts or sprinkling baking soda inside periodically. Rotate between two pairs of cleats if possible, allowing one to fully dry while you wear the other.
