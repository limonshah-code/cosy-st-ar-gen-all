## Why Is A Frankfurter Called A Hot Dog? Unraveling the Mystery

The hot dog stands as a true American icon. We eat them at ball games, backyard barbecues, and street corners. However, a question often comes to mind: **Why is a frankfurter called a hot dog**? It is a food with deep German roots. Yet, it earned a distinctly American name.

This article explores the fascinating journey of the frankfurter. We will trace its origins in Germany and its migration to the United States. We will examine the various theories behind the "hot dog" name. From early street vendors to popular cartoons, we will uncover the stories that shaped this beloved food's identity. Get ready to dive into a delicious slice of culinary history.

### Takeaway

Understanding why a frankfurter is called a hot dog reveals a blend of culinary migration and popular culture.
*   **German Origins:** Frankfurters originated in Frankfurt, Germany, long before they came to America.
*   **Immigrant Influence:** German immigrants brought these sausages to the U.S., selling them from pushcarts.
*   **The Bun's Role:** Buns became standard for serving sausages, making them easier to eat.
*   **Etymological Theories:** The "hot dog" name likely came from the sausage's resemblance to a German dachshund dog. Cartoonists and vendors may have popularized the term.
*   **Cultural Staple:** The hot dog evolved into a quintessential American street food and cultural symbol.

### The Concise Answer

A frankfurter is called a hot dog due to a popular theory linking the sausage to German dachshunds. Early street vendors sold "dachshund sausages," which were often hot. A cartoonist may have depicted a barking dachshund in a bun, labeling it "hot dog" because he could not spell "dachshund." This catchy term quickly spread, forever changing the name of this humble sausage.

### The German Roots of the Frankfurter Sausage

The story of the hot dog begins in Germany, specifically in the city of Frankfurt. Here, the "frankfurter" sausage has a long and proud history. These sausages were traditionally made from pork, sometimes mixed with beef, and smoked over beechwood. Frankfurt citizens have enjoyed them for centuries.

These early frankfurters were a local specialty. They were known for their distinct flavor and slender shape. Records show that frankfurters were already well-established in the 13th century. Some historians claim the first frankfurter was created in 1487, five years before Christopher Columbus discovered America. People traditionally served them with bread and mustard. This established a culinary tradition that later traveled across the Atlantic.

The frankfurter's journey to global recognition began with German emigration. Many Germans sought new opportunities in America. They brought their beloved culinary traditions with them. This migration laid the groundwork for the frankfurter's transformation into the American hot dog. It introduced a tasty sausage to a new land.

### European Sausages Arrive in America: A Culinary Migration

German immigrants played a crucial role in bringing frankfurters to America. They arrived in large numbers during the 19th century. Many settled in cities like New York, Chicago, and Milwaukee. These immigrants often opened butcher shops and food stalls. They sold familiar foods from their homeland.

One of the earliest documented sales of frankfurters in America happened in New York City. A German immigrant, Charles Feltman, began selling them from a pushcart on Coney Island in 1867. He served his sausages with milk rolls and sauerkraut. This quickly became a popular attraction. Feltman's business grew, eventually becoming a large restaurant.

Other German vendors soon followed Feltman's success. They sold "dachshund sausages" or "frankfurters" from their carts. These early sales introduced Americans to the delicious German sausage. The convenience of buying a hot, ready-to-eat meal from a street vendor appealed to many. This marked the beginning of the frankfurter's American journey.

### The Role of the Bun: From Sausage to Sandwich

The way frankfurters were served evolved in America. Initially, vendors often handed customers the hot sausages with gloves. These gloves were sometimes returned after eating. This practice proved unhygienic and impractical. Vendors needed a better solution.

Some accounts credit a German immigrant named Anton Feuchtwanger in St. Louis around 1880 with the invention of the bun. He reportedly lent white gloves to customers to hold his hot "red hots." When customers did not return the gloves, his wife suggested serving the sausages in long buns. These buns were baked specifically for the sausages. This simple change transformed the way people ate frankfurters.

Other stories attribute the bun's popularization to street vendors at baseball games. They needed a convenient way for fans to eat sausages without making a mess. The bun provided a perfect edible holder. This innovation made the frankfurter much easier to consume on the go. It solidified its place as a popular street food.

### The Hot Dog Name: Tracing the Popular Etymology Theories

The most popular theory explaining **why a frankfurter is called a hot dog** involves a cartoonist and a dachshund. Harry Stevens, a concessionaire, sold "Dachshund sausages" at New York polo grounds in 1901. These sausages were long and thin, resembling the German dog breed. He reportedly shouted, "Get your red hot dachshund sausages!"

The story says that sports cartoonist Tad Dorgan was at a game. He saw Stevens' vendors selling these sausages. Dorgan drew a cartoon depicting a barking dachshund inside a bun. He supposedly couldn't spell "dachshund" correctly. So, he simply wrote "Get your hot dogs!" beneath the drawing. This cartoon, though its existence is debated, is widely credited with popularizing the term.

This theory offers a colorful explanation for the name. It links the visual appearance of the sausage to the dog breed. The warmth of the sausage and the familiarity of "dog" as a slang term for something inexpensive or questionable may have also played a role. This etymology quickly captured the public imagination. It made "hot dog" a catchy, memorable name. ### Beyond the Myth: Alternative Theories and Linguistic Evolution

While the cartoonist story is popular, other theories also exist regarding the origin of the "hot dog" name. Some researchers suggest the term might have evolved from earlier slang. The word "dog" was sometimes used to refer to sausages as early as the late 19th century. This was possibly due to skepticism about the meat's quality or simply as a colloquialism.

One theory points to college campuses. Students at Yale University reportedly referred to vendor wagons as "dog wagons" because they sold hot sausages. This term might have stemmed from a humorous or derogatory association with the sausage's content or shape. A Yale student publication from 1895 already used the term "hot dog" to describe a "dog wagon" and its contents. This predates the popular Dorgan cartoon story.

Another perspective is that "dog" was simply a general slang term for any suspicious or low-quality meat product. When combined with "hot" (referring to the temperature of the cooked sausage), "hot dog" became a descriptive name. This term quickly spread due to its catchiness and the growing popularity of the food. Regardless of its exact origin, the term "hot dog" became firmly entrenched in American vocabulary.

### The Hot Dog's Cultural Ascent and American Identity

The hot dog's popularity soared in the early 20th century. It became a staple at amusement parks, fairgrounds, and, most notably, baseball stadiums. Iconic locations like Coney Island cemented the hot dog's place in American culture. Nathan's Famous Hot Dogs, established in 1916, became synonymous with the food. Their annual hot dog eating contest further elevated the food's status. The hot dog became more than just a meal; it became a symbol of summer, leisure, and American values.

The hot dog's simple appeal transcends social classes. From street vendors to gourmet restaurants, it is enjoyed by everyone. ### Frankfurter, Wiener, or Hot Dog: Regional and Historical Nuances

The terms "frankfurter," "wiener," and "hot dog" are often used interchangeably today. However, they once had distinct meanings and origins. Understanding these differences adds depth to the story of this beloved food.

*   **Frankfurter:** This name comes from Frankfurt, Germany. Traditionally, a frankfurter is a sausage made primarily from pork, often with a beef component, and smoked. It tends to be longer and thinner than a wiener.
*   **Wiener:** This name comes from Vienna, Austria ("Wien" in German). Wieners are typically made from a mix of pork and beef, or sometimes all beef. They are generally shorter and plumper than frankfurters. The term "wienerwurst" means "Vienna sausage."
*   **Hot Dog:** This is the overarching American term. It refers to a frankfurter or wiener served in a bun, often with condiments. The "hot dog" term effectively became a generic name for these types of sausages when served in a specific way.

Regional preferences still exist. Some areas might prefer a "frank" over a "wiener," or specify all-beef. However, for most consumers, "hot dog" encompasses all varieties. ### FAQ Section

#### What is the difference between a frankfurter and a hot dog?
A frankfurter is a type of sausage originating from Frankfurt, Germany, usually made from pork and beef. A hot dog is the popular American term for any frankfurter or wiener sausage served in a sliced bun. So, a frankfurter is a specific kind of sausage, while a hot dog is how it is typically served in the United States.

#### Did a cartoon character invent the term hot dog?
The popular story credits cartoonist Tad Dorgan with coining the term "hot dog" in a 1901 cartoon. He reportedly depicted a dachshund sausage in a bun. He couldn't spell "dachshund," so he wrote "hot dog." While this story is widely circulated, some evidence suggests the term "hot dog" was used earlier, even by college students, before Dorgan's alleged cartoon.

#### When did hot dogs become popular in America?
Hot dogs gained popularity in America in the late 19th century, particularly through German immigrants selling them from pushcarts. Charles Feltman opened a hot dog stand on Coney Island in 1867. Their popularity soared in the early 20th century, becoming a staple at baseball games and amusement parks.

#### Is a frankfurter always a hot dog?
No, a frankfurter is not always called a hot dog, especially outside of North America. In Germany, it is simply a "frankfurter" or "Frankfurter Würstchen." The term "hot dog" specifically refers to the frankfurter (or wiener) served in a bun, which is an American culinary innovation.

#### Where did the term "wiener" come from?
The term "wiener" comes from Vienna, Austria, which is "Wien" in German. "Wienerwurst" means "Vienna sausage." Wieners are a type of sausage similar to frankfurters but are generally shorter and plumper, often made from a mix of pork and beef.

#### Are hot dogs made from dog meat?
No, hot dogs are not made from dog meat. The name "hot dog" came from the sausage's resemblance to a German dachshund dog. Hot dogs are typically made from various cuts of pork, beef, or poultry, or a combination of these meats. Any concerns about their contents often stem from old slang and not from actual ingredients.
