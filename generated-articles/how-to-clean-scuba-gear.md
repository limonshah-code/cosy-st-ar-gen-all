## Cleaning Your Scuba Gear: Essential Steps for Every Diver

Imagine coming back from an incredible dive, full of amazing sights and experiences. The last thing you want to do is think about chores. However, taking care of your equipment right away makes a huge difference. Knowing how to clean scuba gear properly ensures it lasts longer and keeps you safe on future adventures. It is a simple step that protects your investment and your well-being underwater.

Cleaning your scuba gear after each use removes salt, sand, and other residues. This prevents corrosion and material breakdown. Regular care maintains the functionality of sensitive components, like your regulator and BCD. My guide will walk you through the correct steps for cleaning each piece of your scuba equipment. We will cover everything from wetsuits to dive computers, helping you keep your gear in top condition.

### Takeaway

Keeping your scuba gear clean is easy with the right steps. Here are the main actions:

*   Rinse all gear thoroughly with fresh water immediately after diving.
*   Use mild, dive-specific cleaners for wetsuits, BCDs, and other fabric items.
*   Never use harsh chemicals on any part of your dive equipment.
*   Properly dry each item completely, away from direct sunlight.
*   Store gear loosely in a cool, dry place to maintain its shape and integrity.
*   Perform regular checks and specific care for regulators and electronics.

**To clean scuba gear**, you must rinse all items thoroughly with fresh, clean water after every dive. Use specialized dive gear cleaner for wetsuits and BCDs, focusing on removing salt, sand, and chlorine. Air dry all equipment completely in a shaded area, then store it properly to prevent damage and extend its lifespan.

## Why Proper Scuba Gear Cleaning is Important for Your Dives

Maintaining your scuba gear goes beyond just keeping it looking good. It is a critical part of dive safety and equipment longevity. Saltwater, chlorine, and even fresh water contain minerals and microorganisms. These elements cause damage if left on your equipment. I always tell new divers that a few minutes of cleaning saves hours of repair or the cost of replacement.

Corrosion is a major enemy of dive gear. Salt crystals dry on metal parts, leading to rust and material degradation. This can affect zippers, buckles, and even the internal components of your regulator. Regular cleaning removes these corrosive elements. This simple step prevents serious functional problems. For example, a sticky zipper can ruin a dive trip. A corroded regulator can be dangerous.

Beyond physical damage, hygiene matters too. Your wetsuit, mask, and even your BCD can harbor bacteria and fungi. These can cause skin irritation or infections. Cleaning with appropriate solutions kills these microorganisms. This ensures your gear is not only functional but also sanitary. I personally value a clean mask; a dirty one feels unhygienic on my face.

Cleaning also lets you inspect your gear. When you clean each item, you naturally look it over. You might notice a small tear in your wetsuit or a worn hose on your regulator. Catching these small issues early prevents them from becoming big problems underwater. A quick inspection during cleaning can save your dive or even your life. It gives you peace of mind before your next adventure.

Finally, proper cleaning protects your investment. Scuba gear is not cheap. Each piece represents a significant cost. Extending the life of your equipment through regular cleaning makes economic sense. It means fewer replacements and more money for actual diving trips. I want my gear to last many years, and cleaning makes that possible.

## Gathering Your Supplies: What You Need to Clean Scuba Gear

Before you start scrubbing, you need the right tools and cleaning agents. Using incorrect products can harm your delicate dive equipment. This section outlines the essential items every diver should have for their cleaning routine. Having these items ready makes the cleaning process quick and efficient after each dive. I keep all my cleaning supplies in one dedicated bucket.

First, you need plenty of fresh water. A large rinse tank or a clean tub works best. If you have a garden hose, that is also excellent. The goal is to remove all salt and sand. Running water over your gear ensures a thorough flush. I always make sure the water is not too hot, as excessive heat can damage materials.

Next, choose your cleaning solutions wisely. For wetsuits, BCDs, and other fabric items, use a specialized dive gear cleaner. These solutions are formulated to remove salt, chlorine, and organic residues without harming neoprene or plastic components. They also help eliminate odors and bacteria. Avoid household detergents or bleach, as these are too harsh. They can strip protective coatings or degrade materials.

You will also need a soft brush or sponge. These are gentle enough to remove stubborn sand or grime without scratching surfaces. A toothbrush can be useful for small crevices. I use a separate brush for my gear, not the one I use for household cleaning. It helps avoid cross-contamination with harsh chemicals.

For your mask, fins, and snorkel, a mild soap or baby shampoo works well. This helps remove skin oils and prevent fogging. You can also use specific mask defoggers designed for dive masks. I find a tiny drop of baby shampoo inside the mask works wonders. Just rinse it thoroughly afterward.

Consider a dedicated drying area. This can be a shaded spot with good airflow or a sturdy clothesline. Plastic hangers are better than wire ones for wetsuits, as they prevent creases and rust stains. I have a designated drying rack in my garage specifically for my dive gear. This keeps everything organized.

Lastly, have a clean, dry cloth or towel ready. These are useful for wiping down certain items, especially electronics. They also help ensure everything is completely dry before storage. A microfiber cloth is excellent for dive computer screens. Having everything ready saves time and ensures a complete cleaning process.

## How to Clean Your Wetsuit, BCD, and Exposure Protection

Cleaning your exposure protection is simple, but it requires specific steps to preserve the material. Your wetsuit, drysuit, and BCD are critical for comfort and safety underwater. Proper care extends their life and keeps them performing as they should. I always clean these items first when I get home from a dive.

Start by rinsing your wetsuit thoroughly with fresh, cool water. This step removes external salt, sand, and any residual chlorine. I like to hang my wetsuit on a wide, plastic hanger and let the water run over it for several minutes. Make sure to rinse the inside as well. You can even step into it in the shower to rinse it quickly.

Once rinsed, soak your wetsuit in a tub or large bucket with fresh water and a wetsuit-specific cleaner. Follow the cleaner's instructions for dilution. Gently knead the wetsuit to work the cleaning solution into the fabric. This helps eliminate odors and kill bacteria. I let mine soak for about 15-20 minutes, giving the cleaner time to work.

For your BCD, the process is similar but includes an important internal rinse. First, rinse the outside of the BCD thoroughly with fresh water, just like the wetsuit. Pay attention to all pockets, straps, and buckles to remove sand. Use a soft brush for any stubborn dirt. Then, rinse the inside of your BCD.

To rinse the inside of your BCD, attach the low-pressure inflator hose and depress the oral inflator button. Hold the BCD upside down and fill it about halfway with fresh water. Swish the water around vigorously to clean the interior. Drain the water by holding the BCD upside down and depressing the dump valve or oral inflator button. Repeat this process until the water drains clean. This removes salt crystals from inside the bladder.

After cleaning, hang your wetsuit and BCD to dry. Use a wide, sturdy hanger for the wetsuit to prevent creasing and stretching. Hang the BCD with the low-pressure inflator hose facing down to ensure all water drains completely. Dry both items in a shaded area with good air circulation. Direct sunlight can damage neoprene. Ensure they are completely dry inside and out before storing them. This prevents mold and mildew growth.

## Cleaning Your Scuba Regulators, Gauges, and Dive Computers

Your regulator is the lifeline that delivers air underwater, so its proper care is non-negotiable. Gauges and dive computers also need careful attention. These items contain delicate components that demand a gentle approach. I treat my regulator with the utmost respect during cleaning, knowing my life depends on it.

To clean your scuba regulator, always ensure the dust cap is securely in place over the first stage. This prevents water from entering the delicate internal parts. Water inside the first stage can cause corrosion and malfunction. Even a small amount of moisture can be problematic. I always double-check this cap before any rinsing.

Next, submerge the entire regulator assembly, including the first stage, second stages, and hoses, in a basin of fresh, cool water. Swish it around gently to dislodge salt and sand. Do not press any purge buttons or free-flow the second stages underwater. This can draw water into the regulator. This step ensures thorough rinsing without internal water ingress. For a more detailed guide on how to clean a scuba regulator, you can find specific instructions [here](https://cosyhomecreation.com/how-to-clean-a-scuba-regulator).

After rinsing, remove the regulator from the water. Allow it to air dry completely. Hang it by the first stage, allowing water to drain from the hoses. Do not use compressed air or direct heat to dry it. This can damage internal components. I usually hang mine in a well-ventilated area for several hours, or even overnight, to ensure it is completely dry.

For your gauges and console, a simple fresh water rinse is usually enough. Gently wipe down the surfaces with a soft cloth. Ensure all salt and sand are removed. Check for any cracks or damage to the console housing. I pay close attention to the pressure gauge to ensure no salt residue remains near the swivel.

Cleaning your dive computer requires extra care because it is an electronic device. Most modern dive computers are designed to be rinsed. Submerge it in fresh water and gently rinse it. Do not press any buttons while it is submerged. Pressing buttons underwater can compromise the waterproof seals. After rinsing, pat it dry with a soft towel.

Allow your dive computer to air dry completely. Pay attention to the charging ports or battery compartments. Ensure no moisture remains before storage. I often leave my dive computer out for a full day after rinsing to ensure complete dryness. Proper regulator maintenance and care for electronics will extend their useful life.

## Caring for Scuba Masks, Fins, and Smaller Accessories

Your mask, fins, and snorkel are smaller items, but they are just as important for a comfortable dive. They also require specific cleaning steps. Neglecting them can lead to fogging masks, brittle fins, or uncomfortable snorkels. I always make sure these smaller items get their share of attention after each dive.

Start with your dive mask. Rinse it thoroughly with fresh, cool water immediately after your dive. This removes salt, sand, and any defog solution residue. Pay close attention to the skirt and strap, where salt crystals often hide. I hold my mask under the tap for a few minutes, turning it to rinse all surfaces.

For a deeper clean, especially if you notice a film or odor, wash your mask with a mild soap or baby shampoo. Apply a small amount to the lens and skirt. Gently rub with your fingers or a soft cloth. Rinse it completely until no soap residue remains. This also helps prepare the mask for defogging on your next dive. I find baby shampoo leaves the silicone feeling soft and clean.

Your fins also need a good rinse. Submerge them in fresh water to remove all salt and sand. Pay attention to the foot pocket, as sand can accumulate there and cause chafing. If you have open-heel fins, ensure the straps are also rinsed well. I often swish my fins vigorously in the rinse tank to clean them thoroughly.

If your fins have adjustable buckles, check them for any trapped sand. Use a soft brush to clean around these areas if needed. Avoid leaving fins in direct sunlight for long periods, as UV rays can degrade the material. After rinsing, stand them up or hang them in a shaded, well-ventilated area to dry completely.

For snorkels, rinse the mouthpiece and tube with fresh water. If the mouthpiece feels sticky or looks discolored, you can wash it with mild soap. Rinse the purge valve thoroughly to ensure no sand blocks it. I make sure to clear any water from the snorkel tube before putting it away. This prevents any stagnant water from sitting inside.

Finally, consider your smaller accessories like dive knives, slates, and gear clips. Rinse these items with fresh water. For dive knives, ensure you rinse them, and then dry them thoroughly to prevent rust. Some divers apply a thin layer of silicone grease to the blade for added protection. Store all small items in a dedicated mesh bag to keep them together and allow airflow. This prevents them from getting lost or damaged.

## Drying and Storing Scuba Gear to Prevent Damage

Cleaning your gear is only half the battle; proper drying and storage are equally important. Incorrect drying or storage can lead to mold, mildew, material degradation, and rust. These issues can shorten your gear's lifespan and make it unhygienic. I always dedicate time to these final steps, as they are crucial for protecting my investment.

After cleaning, the first rule is to dry everything completely. Even a small amount of moisture can encourage mold growth, especially in damp environments. Hang your wetsuits and BCDs in a shaded area with good air circulation. Direct sunlight can cause neoprene to fade, crack, and lose its elasticity. I use wide plastic hangers for my wetsuit to avoid shoulder creases.

For your BCD, ensure all water has drained from the bladder. Hang it upside down with the inflator hose open to allow air to circulate inside. You can also inflate it slightly with clean air and leave it for a while. This helps prevent internal moisture. I always give my BCD an extra shake to check for any trapped water.

Regulators and gauges should hang freely. Ensure the dust cap remains on the first stage. Allow gravity to help drain any residual water from the hoses. Do not store your regulator in a sealed container or bag when it is still damp. This traps moisture and promotes corrosion. I let my regulator hang for at least 24 hours to be certain it is dry.

Dive computers and other electronics must be completely dry before storage. Wipe them down with a soft, dry cloth. Pay special attention to any charging ports or battery compartments. Store them in a dry, cool place, preferably in their original cases. This protects them from impacts and dust. I keep my dive computer in a separate dry box.

Once everything is thoroughly dry, it is time for proper storage. Store your gear in a cool, dry place, away from direct sunlight, heat, and harsh chemicals. Avoid stacking heavy items on top of your dive gear. This can cause permanent creases or damage delicate components. I use large plastic bins for my fins and masks.

Wetsuits should hang on wide hangers or be folded loosely to prevent sharp creases. Drysuits should be stored inflated or loosely rolled. Regulators are best stored loosely coiled in a dedicated regulator bag. Avoid tight coiling, as this can stress the hoses. Properly storing your clean scuba gear protects it from damage and keeps it ready for your next adventure.

## Common Scuba Gear Cleaning Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Even with the best intentions, divers can make mistakes when cleaning their gear. These errors can inadvertently cause damage or reduce the lifespan of their equipment. Understanding these pitfalls helps you avoid them. I have learned a few lessons the hard way, so I want to share common mistakes to help you keep your gear in prime condition.

One of the most frequent mistakes is not rinsing gear immediately after a dive. Saltwater and chlorine start working their corrosive magic very quickly. Leaving gear unrinsed allows salt crystals to form and can lead to irreversible damage. My rule is: if I can rinse it at the dive site, I do. Otherwise, it is the first thing I do when I get home.

Another common error is using harsh chemicals or household cleaners. Bleach, strong detergents, and abrasive cleaners are too aggressive for dive gear materials. They can degrade neoprene, break down silicone, and strip protective coatings. Always use dive-specific cleaners or mild soaps. Remember, the goal is to clean, not to destroy. I learned that using dish soap on my mask can sometimes leave a film.

Forgetting to clean the inside of your BCD bladder is a significant oversight. Saltwater can get inside and dry, forming salt crystals that can puncture the bladder. It can also cause the inflator mechanism to stick. Always rinse the inside of your BCD multiple times until the water runs clear. This small step protects a vital piece of equipment.

Improper drying is another big mistake. Storing gear while it is still damp creates a perfect breeding ground for mold, mildew, and unpleasant odors. It also encourages corrosion on metal parts. Always allow all gear, especially wetsuits and BCDs, to air dry completely. Ensure good ventilation and avoid direct sunlight. I once packed a slightly damp wetsuit, and the smell was awful when I opened the bag later.

Pressing regulator purge buttons or dive computer buttons underwater during rinsing is a serious error. This action can force water past seals into the delicate internal mechanisms. Water inside a regulator or dive computer causes severe damage and can be expensive to fix. Always keep dust caps securely on regulators and avoid button presses on submerged electronics. For a complete guide on how to properly clean a scuba regulator, checking specialized resources can prevent this exact issue [by following expert advice](https://cosyhomecreation.com/how-to-clean-a-scuba-regulator).

Lastly, storing gear improperly can lead to deformation or degradation. Piling heavy items on wetsuits or BCDs can create permanent creases. Storing regulators in tight coils can stress hoses. Keeping gear in hot attics or cold garages can shorten its life. Always store gear in a cool, dry place, arranged to maintain its shape. Proper care includes how you put it away. ## FAQ Section

### Q1: How often should I clean my scuba gear?

You should clean all your scuba gear immediately after every dive, especially if you dive in saltwater or chlorinated pools. A thorough freshwater rinse is the minimum requirement. This prevents salt, sand, and chemicals from causing damage or corrosion. Regular post-dive cleaning maintains hygiene and extends the life of your equipment.

### Q2: Can I put my wetsuit in the washing machine?

No, you should never put your wetsuit in a washing machine. The harsh agitation, hot water, and strong detergents can severely damage the neoprene material. This can cause it to lose its flexibility, reduce its insulating properties, and shorten its lifespan. Always hand wash your wetsuit with specialized cleaners.

### Q3: What should I use to clean my scuba mask to prevent fogging?

To clean your scuba mask, use fresh water and a mild soap like baby shampoo. Rub it gently on the inside of the lens and skirt. Rinse thoroughly. This removes oils and residues. For defogging before a dive, use a dedicated defog solution or a small drop of baby shampoo, rubbed in and then lightly rinsed.

### Q4: How do I remove odors from my scuba gear?

To remove odors from your scuba gear, especially wetsuits and BCDs, soak them in fresh water with a dive-specific cleaner or an odor eliminator designed for sports equipment. Ensure thorough rinsing and complete drying in a well-ventilated area. Sunlight helps kill odor-causing bacteria but can damage material, so avoid direct exposure.

### Q5: Is it okay to use silicone spray on my dive gear?

Silicone spray can be beneficial for certain parts of your dive gear, like zippers on wetsuits or drysuits, to ensure smooth operation. However, avoid using it on areas like regulator second stages or O-rings unless specifically recommended by the manufacturer. Always use dive-grade silicone products and apply sparingly.

### Q6: How should I store my regulator after cleaning?

After cleaning and ensuring your regulator is completely dry, store it loosely coiled in a dedicated regulator bag. Avoid tight coiling, which can stress the hoses. Keep the dust cap securely on the first stage. Store the bag in a cool, dry place, away from direct sunlight or extreme temperatures.
